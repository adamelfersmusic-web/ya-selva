## What this one adds

It calls itself a follow-up and refers throughout to "the prior study." Three things
here are genuinely new to the set:

**1. The Great Highland Bagpipe — the strongest European precedent found.** Three
drones all on A, root-and-octave only, **thirdless by construction**. A nine-note
chanter scale that is genuinely Mixolydian, with the high-G ♭7 tuned near a 7:4 harmonic
seventh (~31 cents below equal temperament) — so the flat seventh is load-bearing,
not ornamental. Neither earlier study covered piping in any depth.

**2. Caporaletti's "Blythe was she" — a direct hit on the exact field.** The 1788
Scots Musical Museum tune analysed as **E–F♯–A–B–D with E as tonic**, i.e. exactly
1–2–4–5–♭7, with *emic* evidence: the original print carries an intermittent bass
drone on e.

> **Both earlier studies cite Caporaletti — and neither noticed this.** ChatGPT
> ranked him #16 as "tonal center analytically debated" (evidence B/C); Claude cited
> him only on the Brăiloiu/Arom methodological divide. This study is the first to
> surface that his actual conclusion lands on the target field with independent
> tonic evidence. That is the single most valuable finding across all three.

**3. The neutral third is not the blue third.** In maqam practice the neutral third
(sīkāh; the third of Rāst) is a **stable, named, theorised structural degree** whose
identity the maqam depends on — a *destination*, not a bend. The blues third is a
variable inflection over an absent or ambiguous third. Both leave major/minor
undecided, by opposite means. This extends the whole project's frame from *absence*
of the third to *substitution* for it.

## It corroborates ChatGPT's rotation arithmetic

Worth flagging given the error noted in doc 14. This study independently computes the
same math and gets it right:

```
G-scale pentatonic G A B D E, centred on G   =  1 2 3 5 6   (major pentatonic)
the SAME pitches relative to A               =  1 2 4 5 ♭7  (the target field)
```

Its sharp observation: on the pipes the exact target field arises **in the
drone-anchored A-centred default**, and the moving-centre tunes recolour *away* from
it. So "fixed field, moving centre" is real, but it moves you off the target, not
onto it.

## Its own negative findings

Unusually disciplined about what it refuses to claim:

- **Arabic maqam is largely irrelevant** to the pentatonic question and should not be
  forced. It contributes the neutral third, the indigenous tonic vocabulary
  (shāhed / īst / āghāz / forūd; karar / güçlü), and the ghammāz 4th/5th pivot.
- **Uilleann pipes are not thirdless as an instrument.** The drones are, but the

- **The Highland "moving tonic" is usually bipolar oscillation** (double tonic), and

- **The tidy four-mode Ethiopian qenet system is partly a 20th-century construction** —
  the modes are real and used; the closed system is contested by fieldwork.
- **"Celtic modes" popular literature is unsourced**, conflates national traditions,
  and misapplies Greek modal names. Celtic–Eastern genetic links are resemblance, not
  documented transmission.
- **The Turkic pentatonic substrate claim is ideologically freighted** (Kemalist
  nation-building) and contested.

# Thirdless Tonic Gravity and the Field 1–2–4–5–♭7: Evidence from Scottish/Celtic and Arabic/Middle Eastern Modal Traditions

## TL;DR

- **The Great Highland Bagpipe is the strongest European precedent found.** It combines a permanent thirdless drone (three A drones, root-and-octave only, no third), a chanter scale that is genuinely Mixolydian (major with a functionally flat 7th — the high-G ♭7 is tuned near a 7:4 harmonic seventh, ~31 cents below equal temperament), and a documented practice of shifting/oscillating tonal centres (A, G, D, E, B) inside a fixed nine-note pitch field — a “fixed field, moving centre” model with a fixed thirdless drone. Independently, Caporaletti’s analysis of the Scottish pentatonic air “Blythe was she” lands on the exact target field E–F♯–A–B–D with E as tonic.
- **The Arabic/Turkish/Persian neutral third is NOT the blues blue third.** It is a stable, named, theorised structural scale degree (sīkāh; the third of maqam Rāst, the second of Bayātī) whose identity the maqam depends on — the opposite of a variable expressive bend over an absent third. This is the single most useful conceptual contribution of the Middle Eastern material, and it argues *against* forcing maqam into the pentatonic/thirdless-tonic frame.
- **The Ethiopian qenet system and the Byzantine ison / Armenian dam are the strongest genuinely-modal, tonic-anchored cases in the Afro–Middle-Eastern region.** Qenet gives four named pentatonic modes with centres set by krar/masenqo drone-strings and phrase finals, and at least one variant (Yematbela Wofe, C–D–F–G–B♭ = 1–2–4–5–♭7) matches the target field exactly. None of this overturns the prior study’s central conclusion; it strengthens it.

## Key Findings

1. **Highland piping is a documented case of permanent thirdless drone + structurally flat 7th + moving tonal centres in a fixed field.** [A] The standard instrument has three drones (one bass, two tenor) all tuned to A — bass one octave below the chanter’s Low A, the two tenors one octave below Low A — pure root-and-octave, with no third anywhere in the drone stack. The chanter scale is Low G, A, B, C♯, D, E, F♯, high G, high A: an A-Mixolydian scale (major with a flat 7th). Ewan Macpherson’s acoustic data (University of Western Ontario) give the modern “harmonic” high-G ♭7 at a 7:4 ratio: in his words, “High Gs are also tuned much flatter, apparently at a ratio of 7:4, which might be termed the ‘Harmonic’ high G since in this case the note’s harmonics coincide exactly with every 7th harmonic of the bass drone.” The 7:4 seventh is 968.8 cents — 31.2 cents below the ET G — so the flat seventh is functionally load-bearing, not an ornamental accident.
1. **The pipe scale is deliberately engineered to yield multiple pentatonic scales, and ceòl mòr exploits shifting centres against the fixed A drone.** [A/B] Seumas MacNeill argued the nine-note scale’s augmented-fourth/flat-seventh design is “a device to produce as many pentatonic scales as possible.” MacNeill and Roderick Cannon identify three principal pentatonic scales (conventionally the G, A and D scales); as Ewan Campbell summarises Cannon, “A (the pitch of the drones) is the only tonal centre that can fit into all three scales. Cannon sees these scales as allowing tunes to have ‘double tonic’ structures… Each has four possible centres, but in practice most tunes only use two.” Barnaby Brown goes further, discarding the single “tonic” for “tonal dynamism.”
1. **Caporaletti’s Scottish melody independently produces the exact target field.** [A] His 2020/21 paper (RJMA – Journal of Jazz and Audiotactile Music Studies, no. 2) analyses “Blythe was she” (Scots Musical Museum No. 180, 1788), the tune Helmholtz printed as a pentatonic example and Brăiloiu later contested. Caporaletti concludes it has a single clear modal centre, E, on the pentatonic mode E–F♯–A–B–D (“no third nor sixth, but with a fourth and a minor seventh”) — i.e. 1–2–4–5–♭7 in E. His decisive evidence is emic: the bass staff in James Johnson’s original print carries an intermittent drone on e (alternating with d), which he treats as insider testimony from cultural insiders (Johnson and Burns) that the tune is E-centred.
1. **The neutral third is a stable structural degree, categorically different from the blues blue third.** [A] It was formally addressed in the proposal at the 1932 Cairo Congress to standardise Arabic tuning to “24 equal steps per octave including quarter tones.” Historically it is the “zalzalian” third attributed to Manṣūr Zalzal, described by al-Fārābī as 27:22 (≈354.5 cents) and by Ibn Sīnā (Avicenna) as 39:32 (≈342.5 cents). Its identity defines the maqam (Rāst, Bayātī, Sīkāh); it is a named destination, not a passing bend.
1. **Indigenous tonal-centre concepts confirm the prior study’s non-triadic mechanisms.** [A] Persian shāhed (prominent/witness tone), īst (stopping tone), āghāz (starting tone) and forūd (cadential descent) explicitly separate the functions Western theory fuses into “tonic.” Turkish karar (final/resting tone) and güçlü (dominant) do the same. These are documented indigenous frameworks for establishing a centre by phrase-final behaviour, register and repetition — not by harmony.
1. **The ghammāz pivot frequently falls on the 4th or 5th.** [A] Maqamat are built from stacked ajnās (tetra-/penta-chords); the ghammāz (often the 4th or 5th degree) is the pivot for modulation and secondary resting. Maqam Rāst modulates to a jins Rāst on the 5th (G over C). This is a genuine analogue to the prior study’s interest in fourth-/fifth-related secondary poles, achieved without triads.
1. **Ethiopian qenet is the strongest genuinely-pentatonic, genuinely-modal regional case.** [A/B] Four principal modes — as Grove/Wikipedia state, “Tizita, Bati, Ambassel, and Anchihoye… Three additional modes are variations on the above: Tizita minor, Bati major, and Bati minor” — all pentatonic, with named tonal centres. Instruments establish the centre by drone-strings and ostinato: the krar’s tonic strings are plucked as a drone while others are muted;  masenqo and bass hold a drone on the tonic. At least one qenet variant equals the target field exactly.

## Details

### PART 1 — Scottish, Irish, Celtic

**1a. Highland bagpipe scale, drones, intonation.** [A] Ewan Macpherson’s published measurements document that the nominal “A” has drifted sharp across the 20th century: “Published measurements from 1885 show the pitch of one chanter to be 441 Hz, while the average of several chanters was found to be 459 Hz in the mid 1950s. Recent measurements show that modern chanters tend to tune between 470 and 480 Hz for low A” — well above concert B♭ (466 Hz). The scale is not equal-tempered, and several degrees sit far from ET. Using Macpherson’s ratios (cents from Low A): B 9:8 (+3.9 vs ET), C♯ 5:4 (−13.7), D just 4:3 (−2) or the sharper 27:20 (+19.6), E 3:2 (+2), F♯ 5:3 (−15.6), high G “harmonic” 7:4 (−31.2). The sharper D (27:20) and high-G (9:5) values come from the 1954 study by J. Lenihan and Seumas MacNeill of 18 pipe chanters; modern soloists tend to the flatter just D (4:3) and harmonic high G (7:4). The consistent finding: the third and seventh sit measurably away from ET, and the modern high-G ♭7 tends flat toward a 7:4 harmonic seventh. The drone is purely root-and-octave (thirdless), so it commits to neither major nor minor. This is the crux: piping is a living tradition in which tonic is fixed by a continuous thirdless drone, the pitch field is fixed, and the melodic centre moves.

**Model A confirmation.** [A/B] Cannon’s three scales, with F♯ and C♯: A-scale (A B C♯ E F♯; possible centres A, E, B, F♯), D-scale (D E F♯ A B; centres D, A, E, B), G-scale (G A B D E; centres G, D, A, E). “Cabar Feidh” is a G-scale double-tonic tune beginning phrases on G (with a third part moving A↔D); “Duncan MacRae of Kintail’s Lament” (ceòl mòr) is documented moving between D and A pentatonic centres phrase by phrase, ending with a touch of G — all against the fixed A drones. Note the important scholarly disagreement: piping analysts increasingly describe this as *oscillation/double-tonic* rather than a single stable non-A tonic. Barnaby Brown explicitly rejects the single-tonic model (“the biggest leap I’ve taken… is to discard the concept of ‘tonic’ or tonal centre, replacing it with tonal dynamism”; the striking behaviour is “ending on a pitch we don’t expect”). So the honest reading is: fixed thirdless drone + fixed field + genuinely mobile tonal focus — but the mobility is often bipolar oscillation, not a clean single relocation of tonic.

**The G-scale caveat re the target field.** The G-scale pentatonic G A B D E, centred on G, is 1–2–3–5–6 (major pentatonic), NOT 1–2–4–5–♭7. Relative to A (drone/centre), the *same* pitches A B D E G give 1–2–4–5–♭7 — the target field. So Highland piping most nearly yields the target set when A is the tonal centre and G functions as the flat 7th, which is the default state, not a relocation. This is significant: the exact target field arises precisely in the drone-anchored default, and the “moving-centre” tunes move *away* from it — a useful compositional distinction.

**1b. Gapped/pentatonic song; the collector problem.** [A] Annie Gilchrist’s five-mode pentatonic classification (adopted by Cecil Sharp) is the foundational scheme; Gilchrist noted heptatonic modes arise by inserting variable auxiliary tones (a variable e could throw an Ionian tune into Mixolydian, and vice versa). The documented collector problem: 19th/early-20th-century collectors key-signed modal/gapped tunes “as if major,” obscuring the real final and sometimes editorially “correcting” flat sevenths. Caporaletti shows Brăiloiu himself argued from a mis-transposed, mis-key-signed (three-flat) transcription. Caution: this compromises any argument resting on printed notation of that era — the pitch inventory may be trustworthy, but the assigned tonic frequently is not.

**“Celtic modes” popular-literature problem.** [A, negative] Much online writing (“The Four Celtic Modes” = Ionian, Mixolydian, Dorian, Aeolian, “the white notes starting on C, G, D, A”) is unsourced, conflates Irish/Scottish/Welsh/Breton/Manx, and misapplies Greek/church modal names. Reliable scholarship (Gilchrist, Sharp, Cannon, Bronson) supports the narrower, evidenced claim: Scottish/Irish traditional melody is heavily pentatonic/hexatonic, its gaps are usually minor thirds (never larger), and Mixolydian/Dorian are common — but the romantic “ancient Celtic mode” framing and Celtic–Middle-Eastern/Indian genetic links are not evidenced (superficial resemblance, not documented transmission).

**1c. Gaelic psalm singing, waulking songs.** [A/B] Hebridean Gaelic psalmody (“precenting the line”/lining out, Lewis and Harris) is unaccompanied free heterophony: a precentor gives the line, the congregation follows at individually varying speeds with dense, pibroch-influenced ornamentation, producing a “shimmering” texture (Calum Martin’s Salm recordings) where a tonal centre is present but there is no fixed drone and no stable third — the melodic outline is heavily melismatic. It is directly relevant to the prior study’s “tonic by repetition/phrase-final + heterophonic density” mechanism, not to drone or to the target field. Waulking songs (òrain luaidh) and puirt à beul are cyclic call-and-response forms relevant to circular (vs directional) melody.

**1d. Other Celtic drone instruments; the uilleann regulator negative finding.** [A] Uilleann pipes: three drones all tuned to D (root/octave — thirdless), BUT the three regulators DO supply actual chords via keys — Tenor C B A G F♯, Baritone A G F♯ D, Bass C B A G, played as two-note “chords” (e.g. tenor+baritone, baritone+bass). This undercuts any “purely thirdless” claim for the full uilleann set: the regulators can and do sound thirds and triadic fragments (the D–F♯ major third is available on the baritone). The drones alone are thirdless; the instrument as a whole is not. Scottish smallpipes and Northumbrian smallpipes carry drones; fiddle cross-tunings (AEAE, ADAE) create sympathetic open-string drones that are root-and-fifth/root-and-octave (thirdless).

**1e. Contemporary Celtic drone-folk.** [B/C, modern] Lankum, The Gloaming and comparable artists build sustained harmonium/shruti/uilleann drones and frequently withhold thirds to keep major/minor undecided; tonic is established purely by the continuous drone and melodic gravity. Clearly modern revival practice, not traditional precedent — included to show the target sound is being actively pursued today.

### PART 2 — Arabic / Middle Eastern / Mediterranean

**Framing (negative finding, as expected).** [A] Arabic maqam is heptatonic, microtonally inflected, largely non-drone and heterophonic. It is NOT a pentatonic tradition and does not yield 1–2–4–5–♭7 as a scale. Its value to this study is conceptual, in three places: (i) the neutral third as stable structural degree; (ii) indigenous tonic vocabulary (shāhed/karar/etc.); (iii) the ghammāz fourth/fifth pivot.

**2a. The neutral third vs the blue third.** [A] In maqam Rāst (C, D, E‑half‑flat, F, G, A, B‑half‑flat) the neutral third is a named, theorised, comparatively stable degree; Scott Marcus’s fieldwork (“The Interface between Theory and Practice: Intonation in Arab Music,” 1993) documents that although the exact intonation varies by region, maqam and performer (it is not exactly 350 cents), the tonic pitch itself is stable and the neutral third is treated as a real scale step, not a glide. Contrast the blues blue third: a variable, bent inflection over a foundation whose third is often absent or ambiguous. **Conclusion, stated directly: these are different phenomena.** For a composer, the maqam neutral third models “the third as a stable *destination* that is neither major nor minor,” whereas the blues third models “the third as an expressive *bend* over an undecided foundation.” Both leave major/minor undecided — but by opposite means.

**2b. Drone / ison / dam.** [A] Byzantine chant: the ison is a sustained vocal drone (isokratima) holding the keynote/tonic of the mode’s main tetrachord — one psaltis described it as “a floor upon which the melody dances,” and melodies typically conclude on the ison pitch. The ison can MOVE to follow modal shifts (its mobility has increased in modern practice), making it a drone that tracks the mode — distinct from the static Highland drone. It is thirdless (a single held pitch). Armenian duduk: played in pairs, the second player (damkash) holds the dam, a sustained tonic drone on a single note (usually the tonic) via circular breathing; the melody duduk moves above it. Both the ison and the dam are the strongest Middle-Eastern cases of “tonic established by a sustained thirdless drone.” Persian tār/setār and Turkish bağlama reiterate open strings as drones: bağlama düzeni ≈ A–D–E and bozuk/kara düzen ≈ A–D–G (root, fourth, and either fifth or ♭7) — the A–D–G tuning literally sounds root/fourth/♭7, thirdless.

**2c. Tonal-centre concepts and modulation.** [A] Persian: shāhed (prominent tone, can shift within a dastgāh), īst (stopping tone), āghāz (start), forūd/khatemeh (cadential goal) — these separate functions that Western “tonic” conflates. As one radif corpus study notes, “the shāhed can shift or be re-emphasized within a dastgāh/āvāz performance… Persian music distinguishes between the shahed (point of focus), Ist (stops…) and Khatemeh (cadential notes), while in Western theory, the tonic usually plays all three roles.” Turkish: karar (final/resting), güçlü (dominant, often the jins-boundary 4th/5th), yeden (leading tone). Modulation: Arabic modulation to a new maqam on the ghammāz, and Turkish şed (transposition), are analogues — but note the distinction: maqam modulation usually TRANSPOSES the modal material to a new tonic (closer to “Model B,” field moves with the centre) OR grafts a new jins onto a pivot; it is not typically the Highland “one fixed field, moving centre” (Model A). The two traditions instantiate different models: piping = Model A; maqam modulation = closer to Model B / jins-grafting.

**2d. Regional pentatonicism.**

- **Ethiopia (high priority).** [A/B] Qenet: tizita, bati, ambassel, anchihoye, all pentatonic, named modes with tonal centres.  Reported interval structures (from C): Ambassel C–D♭–F–G–A♭ (1–♭2–4–5–♭6, a Hirajoshi/Phrygian-pentatonic type); a second “Ambassel/Mixolydian-pentatonic” C–D–F–G–A (1–2–4–5–6); Yematbela Wofe C–D–F–G–B♭ = 1–2–4–5–♭7 — described by Timothy Johnson (FSU World Music Online) as “the same scale that we know of as the Dorian pentatonic scale,” essentially the target field; tizita “major” ≈ 1–2–3–5–6; tizita “minor” ≈ 1–♭3–4–5–♭7 (minor pentatonic, the “Ethiopian blues”). Tonic is established by krar tonic-string drone (the six-string krar in tizita is tuned C D E G A C, its spare string on the tonic octave used as a drone; one source: “if the krar has 6 strings, the remaining string is tuned in an octave to the tonic note”), masenqo/bass drone on the tonic, phrase finals, and slow rubato ostinato.  **IMPORTANT scholarly caution (negative finding):** at least one detailed study argues the neat “closed system of four pentatonic qenet” is a mid-20th-century musicological construction — “since the 1960s, a kind of musicological gospel claims that Amhara secular music is based, supposedly from time immemorial, on a so-called closed system of four pentatonic modes… whereas azmaris of the deep countryside have simply no knowledge about it.” Treat the named modes as real and used, but the tidy four-fold system as partly constructed.
- **Gnawa (Morocco).** [A/B] The guembri/sintir is a three-string skin lute: lowest string a drone, a high string tuned an octave above (unfretted drone), a middle/third string a fourth (or fifth) above the drone — so the open strings sound root, fourth, octave (thirdless). Common keys D and G; patterns are largely pentatonic; the cyclic, ostinato-driven, trance structure (the maalem’s “deep, hypnotic drone that serves as the ceremonial anchor”) parallels the prior study’s cyclic traditions. A strong candidate for cyclic thirdless-tonic practice.
- **Turkic pentatonic substrate.** [A, contested] Ahmed Adnan Saygun’s 1936 *Türk Halk Musikisinde Pentatonizm* argued a Central-Asian Turkic pentatonic root for Turkish folk music; Bartók engaged with it during his 1936 Anatolian trip. Modern scholarship flags this as politically motivated (Kemalist nation-building, “circumventing a Middle Eastern focus”) , and Saygun himself later qualified his position. Report as a contested, ideologically freighted claim, not settled fact.

## Microscope Case Studies

**A. Ceòl mòr — “Cabar Feidh” and “Duncan MacRae of Kintail’s Lament” (GHB).** [A/B] Drone: three A drones, root+octave, thirdless, sounding continuously. Field: fixed nine-note chanter with F♯ and C♯. In “Cabar Feidh” (G-scale, G A B D E), phrases begin and lean on G while the drone holds A — the listener hears G as a temporary centre a whole tone below the drone; the third part shifts toward A↔D. In “Duncan MacRae of Kintail’s Lament,” phrase centres move D→A→D against the same fixed A drone, ending with a touch of G. Third behaviour: no third in the drone; melodic thirds depend on which centre is active. Centre changes: G/A or A/D oscillation. This is the fixed-field/moving-centre model with a permanent thirdless drone — though scholars (Brown) stress it is bipolar oscillation, not a clean single relocation. *(Phrase maps derive from piping-scholarship summaries of Cannon; per-tune tonal-centre labels are analytical and partly contested — graded B where inferred.)*

**B. “Blythe was she” (Scots Musical Museum No. 180, 1788).** [A] Melody: pentatonic E–F♯–A–B–D, no third or sixth. Bass (original print): intermittent drone on e alternating with d. Phrase finals: on e. Third behaviour: none (anhemitonic). Centre: single, E. Caporaletti reads the period harmoniser’s own drone on e as emic proof that the tune is E-centred — the field is literally 1–2–4–5–♭7 in E, with the composer/harmoniser treating E as home. This is the closest documented historical match to the user’s target field with independent tonic evidence. (Caveat below: Caporaletti’s larger polemic against Brăiloiu/Arom is itself qualified by Arom.)

**C. Maqam Rāst performance (neutral third).** [A] Scale C, D, E‑half‑flat, F, G, A, B‑half‑flat; jins Rāst on C plus jins Rāst on G (ghammāz = 5th). The E‑half‑flat is a stable named degree, its exact cents varying by performer/region (Marcus) but never functioning as a glide toward E or E♭. The descent commonly flattens the 7th to B♭. Tonic C is stable; the 5th (G) is the pivot/secondary pole. Third behaviour: present, neutral, structural — the antithesis of the blues bend. No drone (heterophonic ensemble); tonic established by ghammāz pivoting and phrase-final return.

**D. Armenian duduk + dam (e.g. Djivan Gasparyan, *I Will Not Be Sad in This World*).** [A] The melody duduk (commonly tuned to A) moves within a roughly one-octave modal range; the second player (damkash) holds the dam — a single sustained tonic pitch via circular breathing. Third: present in melody, absent from the dam. Tonic: the dam pitch, to which melodic phrases resolve. This is a sustained thirdless drone establishing tonic — but the dam can shift to follow modal changes (“changing the scale harmoniously with the principal duduk”), so it is a *tracking* drone rather than a fixed one.

**E. Ethiopian tizita (azmari with krar/masenqo).** [A/B] Tizita “minor” ≈ 1–♭3–4–5–♭7 (C E♭ F G B♭); the six-string krar in tizita is tuned C D E G A C, with a spare string on the tonic octave used as a drone. Masenqo or bass holds a drone on the tonic; slow rubato ostinato. Third: ♭3 in tizita-minor (or absent in the 1–2–4–5–♭7 Yematbela Wofe). Tonic: reinforced by tonic-string drone, ostinato and phrase finals. Caution: the neat four-qenet system is partly a modern codification.

**F. Contemporary — Lankum (modern drone-folk).** [B/C] Sustained harmonium/shruti and uilleann drones, thirds frequently withheld to keep major/minor open; tonic established purely by the continuous drone and melodic gravity. Explicitly a modern revival aesthetic, not a traditional precedent — included to show the target sound is being actively pursued today.

## Negative / Caution Findings

- **Arabic maqam is essentially irrelevant to the pentatonic/target-field question** and should not be forced. It is relevant only to (i) the stable neutral third, (ii) indigenous tonic vocabulary, (iii) the ghammāz 4th/5th pivot.
- **The neutral third ≠ the blue third.** Stable structural degree vs variable expressive bend — different phenomena.
- **Uilleann regulators supply real chords (including thirds),** undercutting a blanket thirdless-drone claim for the full uilleann set. The drones alone are thirdless; the instrument as a whole is not.
- **The Highland ♭7 IS functionally flat** (high G near 7:4), so that part of the hypothesis holds; but the “moving tonal centre” is often bipolar oscillation (double tonic), and scholars disagree on whether to speak of a single relocated “tonic” at all (Brown rejects it in favour of “tonal dynamism”).
- **The exact target field 1–2–4–5–♭7 arises on the pipes in the drone-anchored A-centred default, not in the moving-centre tunes** — the moving-centre repertoire mostly recolours *away* from that set.
- **The “four-qenet” Ethiopian system is partly a 20th-century construction**; the modes are real and used, but the tidy closed system is contested by fieldwork.
- **“Celtic modes” popular literature is largely unsourced,** conflates distinct national traditions, and misapplies Greek modal names; romantic Celtic–Eastern genetic links are superficial resemblance, not documented transmission.
- **The Caporaletti/Arom pairing is itself qualified:** Arom (in a 2019 email quoted by Caporaletti) distanced his “polarisation” view from Brăiloiu’s flat denial of a tonic, restricting his a-tonality claim to sub-Saharan African pentatonicism. So the “Brăiloiu = Arom” opposition Caporaletti sets up against the jazz/modal view is partly his own construction.
- **The collector problem** means much printed 19th/20th-century Scottish/Irish notation cannot be trusted on tonic assignment, even where the pitch inventory is reliable.
- **Turkic pentatonic substrate** is contested and ideologically freighted.

## Does this change the prior study’s central conclusion?

**It strengthens and refines it.** [Analytical interpretation] The prior study’s framing — “small pitch field + reiterated fundamental + major/minor left undecided,” rather than “the scale 1–2–4–5–♭7” — is vindicated here. Highland piping is the clearest new proof: the drone is thirdless *by construction* (root+octave), which is exactly the physical embodiment of “leaving major/minor undecided while making tonic unquestionable.” The Byzantine ison, Armenian dam, Gnawa guembri and Ethiopian krar all establish tonic without committing the third. The Arabic material adds a genuinely new idea the prior study did not have: a third that is neither major, minor, nor a bend, but a *stable* colour — extending “major/minor undecided” from *absence* (drone/pentatonic) to *substitution* (a fixed neutral degree). And the indigenous vocabularies (shāhed/īst/forūd; karar/güçlü; ghammāz) confirm that non-triadic musicians the world over establish “home” by drone, phrase-final, register and repetition — precisely the mechanisms the prior study identified. The one qualification: “moving tonal centre inside a fixed field” (Model A) is well documented for the pipes but is usually *oscillation* (double tonic), and some serious scholars (Brown) would not call it a “tonic” at all.

## Additional Compositional Principles (CREATIVE EXTRAPOLATION — no claim that historical musicians conceptualised their music this way)

1. **Fixed-drone / moving-centre (piobaireachd model).** Hold a permanent thirdless drone (E, or E+B) and move the melodic centre inside one fixed field — but embrace *oscillation* (bipolar double-tonic) as the idiomatic form of that motion, not just a single relocation.
1. **The neutral third as stable destination, not bend (maqam model).** Treat a fixed neutral third (a real, held ~350-cent tone between G and G♯ in an E field) as a landing note that keeps major/minor permanently undecided — categorically different from bending through the blue third.
1. **The tracking drone (ison/dam model).** Let the drone MOVE to follow the mode/centre rather than staying static — a middle path between a fixed pedal and full modulation.
1. **Phrase-final (karar/īst) tonic establishment.** Establish home purely by where phrases stop and by reiterating a shāhed-like prominent tone, with no drone and no harmony.
1. **Ghammāz fourth/fifth secondary pole.** Build a genuine secondary resting pole on the 4th (A) or 5th (B) of the E field as a pivot, without triadic definition — an I→IV-like move by pivot, not by chord.
1. **Cyclic call-and-response density (waulking / Gaelic-psalm model).** Establish tonic by heterophonic convergence and cyclic refrain repetition rather than by drone.
1. **Separate the tonic’s functions (Persian model).** Deliberately split āghāz (start), shāhed (emphasis), īst (rest) and forūd (final) onto different pitches of the field so “home” is a distributed behaviour, not one note.

## Creative Extrapolation for the E field E–F♯–A–B–D (CREATIVE, kept separate from historical claims)

- **(i) Piobaireachd-style fixed-drone/moving-centre.** Keep a permanent E (or E+B) drone — thirdless, exactly like the A pipe drone. With E as centre the field is 1–2–4–5–♭7. Following Highland double-tonic logic, oscillate the melodic focus to D (a whole tone below the drone, exactly as “Cabar Feidh” leans on G below the A drone) and to A (the 4th, as a ghammāz-like secondary pole), returning to E. Relative to a D focus the same pitches D–E–(F♯)–A–B read as a D-centred major-pentatonic colour; relative to A they read A–B–D–E–(F♯) — so the *same fixed field and drone* recolour without any chord change. This is the most historically-grounded way to realise the user’s system in a European idiom — with the honest caveat that on the pipes the *exact* 1–2–4–5–♭7 belongs to the E-anchored default, and the moving-centre passages recolour away from it.
- **(ii) Stable neutral third between G and G♯.** Instead of a Highland-style absent/variable third, plant a *stable* neutral third (~350 cents above E) as a named landing note. Following the maqam model, this makes the third a destination that is neither major nor minor — shifting the emotional function of the third from “expressive bend/ambiguity” (blues) to “settled, non-committal serenity” (Rāst-like). It preserves the thirdless-tonic gravity of the drone while adding one stable, undecided colour tone — the single most transferable idea from the Arabic material.

## Methodological Note

Throughout, **DOCUMENTED FACT** is carried by the evidence grades: **A** = published transcription, peer-reviewed intonation analysis, documented tuning, or authoritative theory (Macpherson’s measurements; Cannon/MacNeill’s scales; Caporaletti’s primary-source reading; Marcus on maqam intonation; the Cairo Congress record; instrument tunings for duduk, ison, guembri, krar). **B** = strong inference (per-tune piobaireachd centre labels; qenet interval structures from teaching sources corroborated by scholarship). **C** = suggestive analogy or contested claim (Turkic substrate; modern drone-folk as “precedent”). **ANALYTICAL INTERPRETATION** (e.g. reading double-tonic oscillation as “Model A,” or mapping mechanisms onto the prior study) and **CREATIVE EXTRAPOLATION** (the two sections above) are labelled as such and make no claim about historical intent. Cross-cultural resemblances here are treated as **independent emergence** unless documented otherwise; no evidence of transmission between Celtic and Middle-Eastern traditions was found, and romantic claims of such links are flagged as unsupported.
