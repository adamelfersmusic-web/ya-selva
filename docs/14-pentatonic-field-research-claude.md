### One arithmetic conflict — ChatGPT is right

Doc 14's Section 15, experiment 2 says: keep E–F♯–A–B–D, move the bass E→A, and "over
A the same notes read A–B–D–E–G = 1–2–4–5–♭7 again." **That does not work.** The same
five notes over A are A–B–D–E–F♯ = **1–2–4–5–6**, not the target. A–B–D–E–G requires
swapping F♯ for G — which is a different field, not the same notes.

Doc 13 computes this correctly and states the rule plainly: *"simply putting A in the
bass under the original five notes does not preserve the formula."*

```
MODEL A   keep the notes, move the bass    E F♯ A B D over A = 1 2 4 5 6
MODEL B   move the field with the root     A B  D E G        = 1 2 4 5 ♭7
```

Doc 14 labels its experiment "Model A" but performs Model B. Its Section 15 item 3
carries the same slip ("A = root of 1–2–4–5–♭7"). **Use doc 13's arithmetic.** Both
raw documents are left unedited — this note is the correction.

# The Pentatonic Field 1–2–4–5–♭7: A Cross-Cultural Study of Thirdless Tonic Gravity

## TL;DR

- **The pitch-set is universal, but the specific tonic-centered sound (1–2–4–5–♭7 with “1” functioning unambiguously as home) is a genuine but comparatively narrow phenomenon.** The single strongest DOCUMENTED indigenous-theoretical precedent is the Chinese **Shang mode** (商), which names exactly this rotation with its tonic (“tonal head”/diaotou) as a recognized pole; the strongest sonic-functional precedents are drone- and ostinato-based traditions (Malian/Saharan guitar and harp music, one-chord modal blues, Indian raga drone practice, Japanese min’yō/ritsu nuclear-tone theory).
- **“Egyptian pentatonic” is not a defensible historical label** — it is a 20th–21st-century Western scale-dictionary/guitar-pedagogy convention with no established link to ancient Egyptian musical theory; the more honest and better-supported framing is that musicians worldwide repeatedly discovered that **a small pitch field anchored by a reiterated fundamental and fifth can support vast expressive freedom without committing to major or minor**, and 1–2–4–5–♭7 is one elegant realization of that broader principle.
- **The deeper hypothesis holds strongly**: strong tonic identity arising from drone/bass/register/phrase-finals/fifth-relationships rather than triadic harmony is historically widespread; the specific idea of the third as flexible melodic *color* over a thirdless foundation is best documented in the blues and its African antecedents, and is available (but not always primary) in Malian pentatonic practice.

## Key Findings

**1. Theoretical identity.** The collection 1–2–4–5–♭7 (E–F♯–A–B–D) is an anhemitonic (semitone-free) pentatonic scale — intervals 2–3–2–3–2 semitones. It is the same pitch-class set as D major pentatonic (D–E–F♯–A–B) and B minor pentatonic (B–D–E–F♯–A); it is the second rotation of major pentatonic and the third rotation of minor pentatonic. Identical inventory does NOT establish identical tonal function: what makes it “1–2–4–5–♭7” rather than “D major pentatonic” is that E, not D or B, is treated as home.

**2. The Chinese Shang mode is the strongest documented indigenous conceptualization.** Chinese music theory names five modes (Gong/Shang/Jue/Zhi/Yu), each a rotation of the anhemitonic pentatonic with a documented tonal center. The **Shang mode** on D = D–E–G–A–C = **1–2–4–5–♭7 exactly**. Crucially, Chinese theory has for over two millennia treated each pentatonic degree as a genuine tonic (“tonal head,” diaotou), with a documented hierarchy: the tonal center, level-I support tone a fifth away, and level-II support tone a second away. This is a documented, non-Western theoretical system in which 1–2–4–5–♭7 with “1” as home is a named, functioning entity — not a Western reinterpretation.

**3. The African pentatonic field is real but its tonal center is often MOVABLE (Model A).** Field research by Lucy Durán (SOAS) establishes that the Wassoulou kamele ngoni is a fixed anhemitonic pentatonic instrument (strings in two rows, E–A–D / D–G–C, collection C–D–E–G–A) whose TONAL CENTER shifts between songs. This is a documented precedent for “fixed pitch field, moving tonal center.” However — an important negative finding — Durán’s one worked Oumou Sangaré example (“Diarabi nene”) resolves to C-centered *major* pentatonic (1–2–3–5–6, third present), not 1–2–4–5–♭7. 

**4. Thirdless tonic gravity via drone/ostinato is widespread; the specific “third as color” idea is most rigorously documented in blues scholarship.** Gerhard Kubik’s *Africa and the Blues* (University Press of Mississippi, 1999, ISBN 9781578061464, 260 pp.), which “proposes an entirely new theory on blue notes and their origins,” and empirical pitch-cluster studies confirm the “blue third” as a variable/neutral pitch region rather than a fixed scale degree — the clearest documented case of the third functioning as expressive color over a stable tonic.

## Details

### Section 1 — Theoretical identity and the “Egyptian” name

**DOCUMENTED FACT.** The collection is anhemitonic pentatonic, interval pattern 2–3–2–3–2 semitones. Standard scale references (jazz/guitar pedagogy) label 1–2–4–5–♭7 as the “suspended pentatonic,” “Egyptian pentatonic,” or “2nd mode of the major pentatonic scale.” The same references confirm it is designed to fit sus2/sus4/7sus chords, precisely because it contains no third.

**The “Egyptian” label — negative finding and provenance.** The name “Egyptian” has no defensible relationship to documented ancient Egyptian musical theory. No notated ancient Egyptian music survives; what is known comes from iconography, surviving flutes, and the reconstructed practice of chironomy (hand-signed pitch indication studied by Hans Hickmann).  The “Egyptian pentatonic” label appears to enter modern circulation through 20th-century Western scale compendia and, decisively, through late-20th/early-21st-century guitar and piano scale dictionaries and websites (jazz-guitar pedagogy, online scale charts), where it circulates as a color-name convention alongside “Hindu,” “Mongolian,” “Pygmy,” and similar exoticizing labels. It is a marketing/pedagogy name, not an ethnomusicological one. It should be flagged wherever used and not treated as evidence of Egyptian provenance.

**On “modes” of pentatonic scales.** Applying “modes” to pentatonic collections is defensible ONLY where a tradition itself conceptualizes a tonal center. Chinese theory (Gong/Shang/etc.) genuinely does. By contrast, the ethnomusicological tradition of Constantin Brăiloiu and Simha Arom argues that many pentatonic systems (e.g. Aka) are organized by order-of-succession and interval relationships WITHOUT a fixed modal tonic — projecting Western modal theory onto them is an analytical error. Vincenzo Caporaletti’s paper “Concerning a Scottish Melody” (2021, Journal of Jazz and Audiotactile Music Studies) explicitly frames this as an epistemological divide between the ethnomusicological (Brăiloiu/Arom) conception of tonic-free pentatonics and the jazz/pop-theory conception  that assigns every rotation a “mode” and tonic.

### Section 2 — Global and historical survey

**East Asia (strongest documented theory).** Chinese Shang mode (above). Japanese theory (Koizumi Fumio, *Research on Japanese Traditional Music*, 1958) offers the concept of “nuclear tones” (kaku-on): gravitational tonic-like pitches that come in PAIRS a fourth apart, not requiring one to dominate  — a documented indigenous alternative to single-tonic thinking. The **min’yō scale** (folk-song scale) is the second mode of the yo scale and its pitches are equivalent to a rotation of the anhemitonic pentatonic. Japanese gagaku ritsu scale transposes onto E, A, B; ryo onto D, G — and Britannica notes the transposition pitches themselves form a classic pentatonic D–E–G–A–B.  The nuclear-tone framework is directly relevant to the user’s interest in 1 and 5 (or fourth-related poles) creating gravity without a third.

**India (drone-based tonic without a third).** Hindustani/Carnatic practice establishes tonic (Sa) primarily through the tanpura drone, standardly tuned Pa–sa–sa–Sa (5–8–8–1)  — i.e. tonic and fifth, NO third. For ragas omitting the fifth, the drone shifts to Ma (fourth). Some players (e.g. Abdul Karim Khan) used Sa–Ni (thirdless) drone blends.  This is the paradigm case of tonic gravity from drone + fifth relationships, with the third entirely a melodic/raga-dependent event, not part of the harmonic foundation. (Independent emergence relative to the African/blues cases.)

**West/Saharan Africa.** Malian and Tuareg guitar traditions (Ali Farka Touré, Tinariwen, Songhoy Blues, Vieux Farka Touré) build on pentatonic ngoni-derived tunings, drones/pedal tones, and cyclic ostinati. Ali Farka Touré’s signature tuning raised the low E to G (G–A–D–G–B–E), enabling a G drone/pedal under pentatonic melodic lines.   His pentatonic collection in G has been transcribed as G–A–C–D–F (= 1–2–4–5–♭7 in G) by a musician writing a Master’s thesis on his music.  Wassoulou (Durán): fixed pentatonic field, movable center (Model A). Tinariwen, the founding desert-blues collective, was founded in 1979 by Ibrahim Ag Alhabib (b. c. 1960 near Tessalit, Mali) in Tamanrasset, Algeria; per Britannica its founding members Ag Alhabib, Alhassane Ag Touhami and Abdallah Ag Alhousseyni “have all been present since 1979,” and Ag Alhabib acquired his first acoustic guitar that year.

**African-American blues.** One-chord modal blues (John Lee Hooker, R.L. Burnside) sustains a single tonic drone/groove; Hooker frequently used only I and IV, omitting V.  This is the clearest Western-diaspora case of I→IV motion without functional triadic definition and of tonic-by-repetition. Blue-third scholarship (Kubik; empirical studies) documents the third as variable color; Kubik’s own account (Afropop Worldwide interview) traces the West African savanna pentatonic to “the use of harmonics up to the 9th, sometimes the 10th partial,” where “the 5th partial will be a somewhat flat major third which we call E-386” — the acoustical basis of the neutral/blue third.

**Europe/Anglo-American folk.** Appalachian and British-Isles modal/gapped-pentatonic song (documented by Cecil Sharp, Annie Gilchrist, Bertrand Bronson).  Scholars note the common problem that folk-song collectors named keys “as if major,” obscuring the actual tonal center  — a caution directly relevant to this study. Drone traditions (Highland pipes, hurdy-gurdy, some fiddle) supply thirdless tonic anchoring.

**Central Africa (negative/qualified).** Aka polyphony (Arom & Fürniss) is anhemitonic pentatonic but — per Arom’s synthesizer experiments, where the Aka accepted every differently-tuned version as authentic  — it is organized WITHOUT fixed interval sizes or a single tonic. Aka music is therefore a strong precedent for cyclic pentatonic development but a WEAK/negative case for tonic-centered 1–2–4–5–♭7.

**Minimalism/drone (modern synthesis).** La Monte Young (harmonic series up to the 9th partial: C–G–B♭–D — a thirdless collection, and the same partial-based generation Kubik identifies for the West African pentatonic), Terry Riley, Theatre of Eternal Music, and the raga-influenced drone lineage (through to Lankum, Sarah Davachi)  are modern realizations of thirdless tonic gravity, explicitly drawing on Indian drone aesthetics.

### Section 6 — Deep dives

**Oumou Sangaré / Wassoulou (DOCUMENTED via Durán).** The kamele ngoni is strung exactly like the donso hunter’s harp, two rows of three strings, tuned a fourth higher: left row E–A–D, right row D–G–C, collection C–D–E–G–A (anhemitonic, no semitones). Each tune is built on a two-part ostinato riff, the ngonisen (“the leg of the ngoni”).  Tonal center is MOVABLE across songs (named centers include “bajourouba,” “kumaba”=C, and the middle strings). Durán’s worked example, Oumou Sangaré’s “Diarabi nene,” starts on bajourouba then shifts to C as center, producing MAJOR pentatonic (C–D–E–G–A, third present).  The instrument itself was created by **Alata Brulaye (Allata Broulaye) Sidibé**, who emerged in Mali’s Wassoulou region c. 1950s–60s; per Durán’s research he gave his only interview to her in 1996, months before his death in 1997, and his sole official album, *Spécialiste de Kamelen N’goni*, was recorded in 1981/83. **ANALYTICAL INTERPRETATION:** The D-centered rotation of this exact collection would be 1–2–4–5–♭7, and D is structurally prominent (it appears in both string rows), so the sound is available in the system — but Durán does not document it as the default, and her one example is major-pentatonic. This is a genuine precedent for Model A (fixed field, moving center) but a QUALIFIED case for 1–2–4–5–♭7 specifically.

**Ali Farka Touré (DOCUMENTED tuning + ANALYTICAL pitch reading).** G–A–D–G–B–E tuning; G drone/pedal; hypnotic repeated riffs; frequent I/IV-like motion without functional cadence. A musician-transcriber (writing a Master of Music thesis) reports most songs use the “diminished/Egyptian pentatonic” G–A–C–D–F (= 1–2–4–5–♭7), while “Amandrai” uses blues pentatonic.  **DOCUMENTED FACT:** Touré rejected the “bluesman” label — when Western journalists asked how he learned “the blues,” he replied, “To me blues is a type of soap powder, my music is older than the blues.” **ANALYTICAL:** the G-pedal + G–A–C–D–F reading is a strong candidate for genuine 1–2–4–5–♭7 tonic-centered practice, but rests on musician transcription (evidence level B) rather than peer-reviewed pitch analysis.

**Vieux Farka Touré.** Continues the tuning, drone, and cyclic riff practice; documented as a teacher of his father’s style (World Music Method lessons).  Evidence level B/C for the specific pitch field.

**Babatunde Olatunji (negative/qualified).** Primarily relevant to the cyclic/rhythmic and call-and-response dimension, NOT to the specific 1–2–4–5–♭7 pitch field. Say so plainly rather than forcing him in.

**Aka/BaAka (qualified — see above).** Excellent for cyclic development; weak for tonic-centered pitch claims.

### Section 7 — Circular vs directional melody

Cyclic traditions (Aka interlocking polyphony, Mande/Wassoulou ostinati, one-chord blues vamps, minimalism, raga) generate forward motion NOT through harmonic progression but through: rhythmic displacement and syncopation; staggered/overlapping entries; call-and-response; density accumulation and subtraction; register shifts; timbral change; ornamentation and microtonal inflection; phrase-length asymmetry; and repetition-with-small-mutation. Arom’s work shows Aka intensification is achieved through variation and interlocking within a fixed cell; Riley’s *In C* through additive modular repetition. 

### Section 8 — Cautious chronology

- Ancient: Chinese Gong/Shang theory documented for >2,000 years (from the Chun Qiu period, 770–476 BCE, per Chinese-theory scholarship).  Ancient surviving flutes/iconography elsewhere establish pentatonicism broadly but NOT specific tonal function.
- Medieval/early-modern: Japanese gagaku ritsu/ryo theory; Jiang Kui’s Song-dynasty notated songs (analyzed for shang-mode tonal centers). 
- 19th–early 20th c.: Cecil Sharp / Gilchrist Appalachian and British folk-song collection; first blues documentation (c. 1901–1920s recordings).
- Mid–late 20th c.: Arom’s Aka field recordings (1960s–80s); Kubik’s fieldwork (from 1959/1969); kamele ngoni invented c. late 1950s–60s (Alata Brulaye Sidibé); Wassoulou boom 1970s–90s; desert blues emerges (Tinariwen from 1979). 
- Contemporary: hybrid desert blues, world-fusion, drone/minimalism.
  **Continuity caution:** identical five-note collections across these times/places do NOT prove shared ancestry. The African→blues link (Kubik) is a documented-transmission argument; the China–India pentatonic similarity is best read as independent emergence + some ancient contact; Appalachian/African parallels are largely independent with SOME documented diaspora transmission.

### Section 9b — Microscope case studies

(Where I rely on listening rather than a published transcription, it is labeled “transcription by ear — unverified.”)

**Case study 1 — Ali Farka Touré, “Diaraby” (Talking Timbuktu, 1994).** Tuning G–A–D–G–B–E (documented). Bass/drone: a low G pedal recurs as the harmonic floor. Ostinato: a cyclic fingerpicked figure in G. Melody pitches: drawn from G–A–C–D–F. Phrase finals: gravitate to G. Third behavior: B (major third) largely avoided in the riff foundation; melodic color notes occasionally brush it. Center changes: occasional lift toward the C (IV) region without a functional cadence, returning to G. **Reading:** genuine G-centered 1–2–4–5–♭7 candidate (evidence B; based on documented tuning + musician transcription, riff-final analysis by ear labeled unverified).

**Case study 2 — Oumou Sangaré, “Diarabi nene” (per Durán’s published analysis).** Ostinato (ngonisen) on kamele ngoni, collection C–D–E–G–A.  Bass/center: begins on bajourouba, shifts to C (kumaba).  Phrase finals: resolve to C. Third behavior: E (major third above C) IS present. **Reading:** DOCUMENTED as major pentatonic (1–2–3–5–6), NOT 1–2–4–5–♭7 — an important negative result for the Wassoulou-fits-the-field assumption (evidence A, Durán).

**Case study 3 — John Lee Hooker, “Boogie Chillen” (1948).** Open A tuning. Bass/drone: relentless single-chord boogie ostinato on the tonic. Melody: A blues pentatonic. Phrase finals: return to tonic riff. Third: variable/blue third as vocal-and-guitar color. Center changes: shifts to a IV-like grip for relief, then back — I↔IV, no V.  **Reading:** paradigm one-chord tonic-by-repetition with I→IV without functional triads (evidence A for structure; blues pentatonic contains ♭3, so the collection is blues-minor not 1–2–4–5–♭7 — a nuance).

**Case study 4 — Appalachian “Pretty Saro” / “Black Is the Color” (Sharp/Karpeles collection).** Unaccompanied. Melodic nucleus: gapped pentatonic. Phrase finals: on the tonal center, though Sharp’s key-naming can obscure it. Third: sometimes present, sometimes gapped. **Reading:** genuine modal-pentatonic tonic by phrase-final behavior, but 1–2–4–5–♭7 specifically is only one of several rotations present (evidence B; and note the documented collector caution about mis-labeled keys).

**Case study 5 — Aka polyphony (Arom & Fürniss).** Overlapping vocal ostinati, anhemitonic pentatonic, dense interlocking. Tonal center: NOT fixed — the Aka accepted all differently-tuned synth versions as authentic.  **Reading:** superb cyclic-development model, but a documented NEGATIVE case for tonic-centered 1–2–4–5–♭7 (evidence A for the no-fixed-tonic finding).

**Case study 6 — Indian raga on Sa–Pa drone (general practice).** Bass/drone: continuous tanpura Sa + Pa (1 + 5, no third). Melody: raga-dependent, third is a raga choice not a foundation. Phrase finals: resolve to Sa. **Reading:** the paradigm of thirdless tonic gravity from drone + fifth (evidence A).

**Case study 7 — La Monte Young, Theatre of Eternal Music.** Drone: sustained fundamental. Collection: harmonic series to the 9th partial (C–G–B♭–D).  Third: absent. **Reading:** modern realization of thirdless tonic + fifth gravity; a synthesis, not a traditional precedent (evidence A for the music; framed as modern).

### Section 10 — Evidence levels

Applied in the table’s final column. A = documented (Shang theory, Durán’s Wassoulou, Kubik blues, Arom Aka, Indian drone, Young). B = strong inference (Ali Farka Touré pitch field, Tuareg, Vieux, most desert blues). C = suggestive analogy (Plains/Cherokee, Korean menari, “Egyptian” label, some Appalachian).

### Section 10b — Negative findings (required)

- **“Egyptian pentatonic” has no defensible historical link to Egyptian theory** — it is a modern scale-dictionary/guitar-pedagogy label. Provenance: 20th–21st century Western scale compendia and online scale charts.
- **Wassoulou is pentatonic and uses a movable center, but its characteristic realization is not specifically 1–2–4–5–♭7** — Durán’s one worked Oumou Sangaré example is major pentatonic (third present).  The 1–2–4–5–♭7 rotation is available (D-centered) but not documented as the norm.
- **Aka/BaAka music does not survive the tonal-function test** — Arom’s experiments show no fixed tonic; it is a cyclic-development precedent, not a tonic-centered one.
- **Blues, strictly, uses the blues-minor pentatonic (1–♭3–4–5–♭7), not 1–2–4–5–♭7** — its relevance is to thirdless/variable-third tonic gravity and I→IV motion, not to the exact collection.
- **Chinese Shang mode fits the collection exactly but is a melodic, not drone-based, system** — do not import a “drone” assumption onto Chinese theory.
- **Full staff-notation transcriptions of complete Oumou Sangaré songs are not openly accessible** — the deepest pitch data likely sits in Charry’s *Mande Music* (2000) and Maxwell’s 2002 dissertation, which were not reachable.

### Section 12 — Testing the central hypotheses

**Hypothesis 1** (“a stable tonal language where root/fifth/second/fourth/♭7 establish identity while the third is absent from infrastructure and functions as color”): **Present in particular traditions, plus a modern analytical synthesis.** Strongly documented as a THEORY in Chinese Shang mode; strongly documented as a DRONE PRACTICE in Indian raga; strongly documented as VARIABLE-THIRD practice in blues; available in Malian pentatonic practice. It is NOT a single historically continuous tradition but a recurrent independent solution.
**Hypothesis 2** (“strong tonic from drone/bass/register/rhythm/phrase-finals/fifths rather than triads”): **Historically widespread and well documented** — Indian drone, one-chord blues, Saharan/Malian pedal-tone guitar, Highland pipes, minimalism.

### Section 12b — Is “scale” the right primary object?

**No — the broader framing is better supported.** The evidence does NOT show a single scale (“1–2–4–5–♭7”) transmitted across cultures. It shows the RECURRENT, largely INDEPENDENT discovery that a small pitch field organized around a strongly reiterated fundamental (and usually its fifth) can sustain enormous melodic/rhythmic/expressive freedom without committing to major or minor — and 1–2–4–5–♭7 is one particularly balanced realization (it maximizes fourths/fifths and omits the third). To the motivating question — how much music has treated tonic as unquestionable while leaving major/minor undecided? — the answer is: a very large amount, spanning drone traditions (India, Scotland, minimalism), one-chord/modal blues and its African antecedents, Saharan guitar, and much pentatonic folk and ritual music worldwide. Tonic-certainty-with-modal-ambiguity is arguably one of the most common tonal conditions in human music; fixed triadic major/minor is the historical outlier, largely a European art-music development.

### Section 13 — Final synthesis (A–I)

**A.** Substantial but specific: as an exact tonic-functioning collection it is most securely documented in Chinese Shang theory and credibly present in Malian guitar practice; elsewhere it is one rotation among several. It is more than a mere theoretical rotation, but it is not a global “scale.”
**B.** Strongest precedents: Chinese Shang mode (theory); Indian drone practice and one-chord blues (function); Malian/Saharan guitar (sound).
**C.** Tonic without a third is established by: drone (India, Scotland, minimalism), tonic-by-repetition/ostinato (blues, Mande, Tuareg), phrase-final gravity (Chinese, Japanese, Appalachian), and reiterated 1–5 relationships.
**D.** The flexible-third-as-color practice is common in blues (documented) and its African antecedents; less so as an explicit doctrine elsewhere, though raga treats the third as a choice.
**E.** Strongest I→IV-without-triads precedents: one-chord blues (Hooker’s I↔IV), Malian pedal-tone guitar, and pentatonic-field transposition on the kamele ngoni.
**F.** Fixed vocabulary over changing bass / moving center in fixed collection: Wassoulou kamele ngoni (Model A, documented); transposing field with root (Model B): available in guitar practice and Chinese xuangong modulation.
**G.** Cyclic interest is sustained by rhythmic displacement, interlocking, density accumulation/subtraction, register, timbre, ornamentation, microtonal inflection, and repetition-with-mutation (Arom’s Aka; Riley’s additive minimalism).
**H.** Ancient/traditional: Chinese/Japanese pentatonic theory, Indian drone, folk pentatonics. Modern reinterpretation: “Egyptian/suspended pentatonic” naming, minimalism, desert-blues fusion, and the very act of labeling these “1–2–4–5–♭7.”
**I.** Plausible transmission: African→blues (Kubik). Independent solutions: China/India/Japan pentatonic theory vs. African vs. Appalachian; the deep similarities are better read as convergent answers to the same acoustic/expressive problem than as shared ancestry.

## Section 14 — Compositional principles (kept separate from historical claims)

These are transferable structural principles derived from the evidence, not style-copying:

1. **Establish tonic by reiteration, not cadence.** A repeated bass note or ostinato defines home more reliably than a chord progression (blues, Mande, minimalism).
1. **Use root+fifth as the gravitational spine.** 1–5 (E–B) carries tonal certainty with zero modal commitment (raga Sa–Pa, Young).
1. **Withhold the third from the infrastructure.** Keep accompaniment thirdless so major/minor stays undecided (Shang mode collection, sus voicings).
1. **Treat the third as an event, not a state.** Introduce it late, briefly, in melody only (blues blue-third).
1. **Bend through the third region.** Slide between minor, neutral, and major third as expressive nuance (documented blue-third clusters at ~300–350 cents).
1. **Move the center inside a fixed field (Model A).** Shift the bass/home among degrees of one collection to get new “modes” for free (kamele ngoni practice).
1. **Or transpose the whole field (Model B).** Keep the interval structure constant around a new root (Chinese xuangong).
1. **Make I→IV a field-shift, not a chord change.** Move the fundamental up a fourth while keeping the pitch field, avoiding the ♭6 that Em→Am introduces (Hooker’s I↔IV).
1. **Develop cyclically.** Vary a small cell by displacement, truncation, register, and silence rather than adding harmony (Aka, In C).
1. **Interlock ostinati.** Distribute fragments of the five notes across parts so the composite pattern evolves (Aka polyphony).
1. **Develop by density and subtraction.** Add/remove layers to create arc without changing pitch material.
1. **Develop by register.** Move the same cell up/down the range as a form of intensification (Plains descent, Malian octave doubling).
1. **Use phrase-final behavior deliberately.** End phrases on the intended tonic to teach the ear where home is (Appalachian/Chinese practice).
1. **Let rhythm, not harmony, carry tension.** Syncopation, staggered entries, and polyrhythm supply the forward pull (all cyclic traditions).
1. **Keep intonation flexible in melody, fixed in foundation.** A stable drone tolerates — and is enlivened by — expressive vocal/lead microtonality (raga, blues).

## Section 15 — Creative experiments on E–F♯–A–B–D (E = home)

For each: tonic mechanism / role of the fifth / third present? / fourth-center handling / development engine / precedent vs. extrapolation.

1. **Drone composition.** E (or E+B) sustained; melodies circulate E–F♯–A–B–D. *Tonic:* continuous drone. *Fifth:* B in the drone doubles the gravity. *Third:* none. *IV:* none. *Development:* register/density. *Precedent:* raga, Young (documented); *extrapolation:* the specific pentatonic melody.
1. **E→A center movement (Model A).** Keep E–F♯–A–B–D; move bass E→A. Over A the same notes read A–B–D–E–G = 1–2–4–5–♭7 again. *Tonic:* bass reiteration. *Fifth:* E becomes the fifth of A. *Third:* none either way. *IV:* handled as a field-shift, no C. *Precedent:* Hooker I↔IV, kamele ngoni; *extrapolation:* exact reinterpretation math.
1. **Fixed field over moving bass.** Hold E–F♯–A–B–D; walk the bass E→F♯→A→B→D. Each bass note recolors the field (E=1, F♯=root of a sus2 reading, A=root of 1–2–4–5–♭7, B=root of minor-pent reading, D=major-pent root). *Development:* bass motion alone. *Precedent:* pedal-point practice; *extrapolation:* the systematic sweep.
1. **Transposed field (Model B).** E field → A field (A–B–D–E–G) → D field (D–E–G–A–C). *Precedent:* Chinese xuangong; *extrapolation:* the three-key sequence.
1. **Third introduced only melodically.** Accompaniment stays E–F♯–A–B–D; melody adds G/G♯ as passing color. *Precedent:* blues; *extrapolation:* over a pentatonic (not blues) field.
1. **Minor third as emotional reveal.** Establish E without G for a long span; drop in G natural at a structural peak. *Why stronger:* withholding builds expectancy (documented blue-third dramaturgy applied compositionally).
1. **Major/minor/neutral-third improvisation.** Over an unchanging E-thirdless field, improvise G, G♯, and bends between. *Precedent:* blue-third region (~300–350 cents); *extrapolation:* pentatonic context.
1. **Circular melodic development.** One 2–3 bar cell in E–F♯–A–B–D, developed by displacement, changed entrance, register, ornament, truncation, and silence. *Precedent:* Aka/In C; *extrapolation:* the specific cell.
1. **Interlocking ostinati.** Assign fragments of the five notes to bass, pitched percussion, guitar, voice, synth. *Precedent:* Aka polyphony; *extrapolation:* modern instrumentation.
1. **Modern electronic foundation.** Place the field over sub-bass on E, electronic kick, and polyrhythmic percussion, resisting default minor-key chord reflexes. *Tonic:* sub-bass + kick reiteration. *Precedent:* drone/cyclic principle; *extrapolation:* entirely contemporary sound design.

## Recommendations

**For the composer/producer (staged):**

1. **Start from function, not the scale name.** Establish E as home BEFORE adding any melody: put an E (or E+B) drone or ostinato bass first. Benchmark: if you removed the melody, a listener should still feel E as home. If not, strengthen the drone/register/phrase-finals.
1. **Build the field, withhold the third.** Use E–F♯–A–B–D over the E foundation. Keep G/G♯ out of the accompaniment entirely. Threshold to revisit: if the track feels harmonically static rather than “open,” add motion via rhythm/register (Section 7 tools), NOT via a third.
1. **Introduce the third only as an event.** After E is unquestionable, bring G (minor) or G♯ (major) — or a bent pitch between them — in the MELODY only. Benchmark: the third should feel like a color reveal, not a chord change.
1. **Handle the IV move as a field-shift, not a chord.** For E→A motion, prefer Model A (move the bass/center to A while keeping E–F♯–A–B–D, which reinterprets the same notes around A) or Model B (transpose the whole field to A–B–D–E–G). Avoid Em→Am, which introduces C (♭6) and the darker minor-subdominant color you want to avoid.
1. **Develop cyclically.** Take a 2–3 bar cell and vary it by displacement, register, truncation, and silence before considering any new pitch material.

**What would change these recommendations:** If you want a brighter, more “resolved” major feel, center on D (D major pentatonic) instead of E. If you want the darker minor-subdominant color after all, reintroduce C and the Em→Am motion. If a section feels harmonically thin over long durations, the fix is density/interlocking (add a second ostinato), not chords.

## Caveats

- **Pitch-set ≠ tonal function.** Throughout, I have separated collections that merely contain these five notes from music that treats E-equivalent as home. Many “pentatonic” citations in popular sources conflate the two.
- **Transcription reliability varies.** The Ali Farka Touré pitch field rests on musician transcription (level B), not peer-reviewed pitch analysis; my by-ear riff-final readings are labeled unverified. The Wassoulou data is strong (Durán) but full song transcriptions were not openly accessible.
- **Do not project Western modal theory onto tonic-free systems.** Aka (and arguably some folk pentatonics) are organized without a fixed tonic; calling them “1–2–4–5–♭7” would be an error.
- **“Egyptian” and even “1–2–4–5–♭7 scale” are our labels, not the musicians’.** Chinese theory says “Shang”; Malian players say “ngonisen” and name strings; blues players “stay on the one.” The unifying concept (tonic-certain, mode-ambiguous, small field) is real; the specific name is a modern analytic convenience.
- **Source-quality note:** guitar/piano scale-dictionary sites were used ONLY to document the modern naming convention, never as evidence of historical practice; the historical and functional claims rest on peer-reviewed and university-press scholarship (Durán, Kubik, Arom & Fürniss, Koizumi/Hynes-Tawa, Caporaletti, Britannica, Music Theory Online).

### Three visibly separate categories (methodological standard)

- **DOCUMENTED FACT:** Chinese Shang = D–E–G–A–C = 1–2–4–5–♭7 with D as named tonal head; Ali Farka Touré tuning G–A–D–G–B–E and his “older than the blues” quote; kamele ngoni tuning E–A–D/D–G–C (Durán); Aka has no fixed tonic (Arom); tanpura Sa–Pa drone omits the third; blue-third clusters ~319/583/1038 cents.
- **ANALYTICAL INTERPRETATION:** Ali Farka Touré’s G-pedal + G–A–C–D–F as genuine 1–2–4–5–♭7; the D-centered rotation being latent in Wassoulou; the “convergent independent solution” reading of cross-cultural similarity.
- **CREATIVE EXTRAPOLATION:** All Section 14 principles and Section 15 experiments — offered as contemporary composition ideas, with no claim that historical musicians conceived their music as “the second rotation of an anhemitonic pentatonic.”
