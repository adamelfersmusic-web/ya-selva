# Functional Harmony in Historical and Cross-Cultural Perspective: Drones, Modes, Stable Pitch Fields, Cycles, and Shifting Tonal Gravity

## Executive conclusion and definitions

### Executive conclusion

The hypothesis is **broadly supported in its central negative claim, but too broad in its positive formulation**.

It is historically defensible to say that **modern Western chord-by-chord functional harmony is a comparatively recent and culturally specific way of organizing pitch**, rather than a universal grammar of music. The strongest historical account places the gradual crystallization of harmonic tonality in Europe across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, with basso continuo after about 1600 helping to foreground bass-supported vertical relationships, and Jean-Philippe Rameau’s 1722 theory providing an especially consequential formulation of chord roots and the *fundamental bass*. Explicit theoretical language equivalent to modern “tonality” and “function” is later still: Alexandre-Étienne Choron used *tonalité* in 1810 in a sense involving tonic, dominant, and subdominant relations, while nineteenth-century theorists—especially Hugo Riemann—systematized the idea of harmonic functions. citeturn3view0turn3view1turn27search0turn27search5turn27search13turn0search2

That chronology does **not** mean that Europeans before 1600 lacked tonal centers, triads, consonance, cadences, bass lines, or purposeful vertical combinations. Medieval and Renaissance polyphony could have remarkably strong final-centered organization and recognizable sonorities. What was different was the *systematic explanatory and compositional priority* given to independent contrapuntal lines, modal categories, cadential structures, solmization, interval treatment, and other procedures rather than to a succession of root-bearing chords performing tonic, predominant, and dominant functions. Dahlhaus's central warning is especially important: the notation of an older vertical sonority does not by itself establish that it was *conceived as a chord* in the later harmonic sense. citeturn3view0turn3view1turn1search5turn1search22turn1search7

Cross-culturally, the evidence is even clearer that functional harmony is only one possible architecture of pitch. Hindustani classical music can organize long performances through rāga, a stable tonic/drone, melodic hierarchy, characteristic phrases, ornamentation, and cyclic tāla without common-practice chord syntax; Arabic maqām organizes melodic pathways, focal pitches, ajnās, and modulation; Central Javanese gamelan differentiates *laras* and *pathet* and creates elaborate cyclic, stratified textures; Mande kora performance uses fixed tunings, repeating *kumbengo*, variation, interlocking and contrapuntal textures; Aka polyphony produces complex vertical results from cyclic, interdependent vocal parts; Appalachian old-time practice extensively exploits drones, resonant open strings, alternate fiddle and banjo tunings, and melody-centered texture. None of these systems is simply a version of the others. citeturn9search1turn11search0turn23search1turn13search3turn25search1turn16search2turn20search15

The hypothesis becomes misleading, however, if it is restated as **“before functional harmony, music everywhere used stable scales over shifting bass roots.”** The evidence does not support such a single ancestral system. In Hindustani music the central tonic is generally exceptionally stable rather than mobile. In maqām, a change in tonal focus can entail a change of *jins* or pitch resources, not merely a reinterpretation of one unchanged collection. Aka polyphony is better understood through interdependent cyclic lines than through a scale-over-bass model. And in Appalachian music, drones and open strings are abundantly documented, whereas the specific idea of deliberately changing “roots” underneath a fixed scale as the primary indigenous harmonic principle is much less securely documented. citeturn9search1turn11search0turn16search4turn20search15turn20search26

The most defensible reformulation is therefore:

> **Functional major-minor tonality—understood as a syntax of root-bearing chords with directed relations to a tonic—is a historically specific European system that consolidated principally during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries and was theorized increasingly explicitly thereafter. Earlier European music and many musical traditions outside that system have instead, or additionally, organized pitch through melodic/modal hierarchies, drones or pedals, restricted or relatively stable pitch inventories, contrapuntal relationships, formulaic melody, cyclic rhythm, ostinati, heterophony, interlocking parts, registral organization, and shifting pitch emphases. These practices are heterogeneous rather than manifestations of one universal “pre-functional” system, and many of them also contain vertical sonorities, triads, bass motion, or modulation without organizing those phenomena according to common-practice functional syntax.**

That is the version best supported by historical theory, ethnomusicology, organology, and perceptual research. citeturn3view0turn3view1turn9search1turn13search3turn25search1turn26search0

### Working definitions

**Functional harmony.** For this report, functional harmony means more than “music containing chords.” It is a syntax in which harmonies are understood as relatively stable chordal entities—normally with roots that persist through inversion—and acquire directional roles by their relationship to a governing tonic: tonic, dominant, and preparatory/predominant or subdominant functions are the canonical example. Cadences, tonicization, modulation, and expectations of resolution emerge from relations among these functions. “Predominant” is especially useful as a modern analytical category rather than something every eighteenth-century musician would necessarily have named that way. Rameau's *fundamental bass* was crucial to making chord roots independent of the actually sounding bass conceptually explicit, while later theorists further developed ideas of scale degree and harmonic function. citeturn27search0turn0search2turn0search22

**Modality.** “Modal” should not mean merely “a major scale starting on a different note.” Historically and cross-culturally it can denote organization through a final or tonic-like pitch, characteristic degrees, registral ranges, melodic formulas, cadential tendencies, permissible pitch inflections, and other rules. Medieval European modes, Renaissance tonal organization, Indian rāga, Arabic maqām and Javanese *pathet* are therefore not interchangeable instances of one abstract modal system. Even within Renaissance scholarship, reducing compositions to a simple list of eight or twelve modes has been challenged by work emphasizing tonal types, cadential planning, hexachordal space, and composition-specific structures. citeturn1search2turn1search7turn1search22turn9search1turn23search1

**Drone.** A drone is a sustained or repeatedly reiterated pitch or pitch-complex that provides an ongoing acoustic reference. A drone may define a tonic, but need not behave like the root of a Western chord. A drone can coexist with consonance and dissonance that would be described quite differently if each vertical moment were forced into chord symbols. Historical instruments such as hurdy-gurdies and some zithers and bagpipes embody the principle physically through dedicated drone strings or pipes. citeturn22search1turn22search5turn22search7

**Tonal center.** A tonal center is a pitch or pitch class experienced or treated as a point of orientation, repose, return, finality, or hierarchy. Having a tonal center does not in itself imply common-practice tonality. A Gregorian final, the tonic maintained by a tānpūrā, a focal note in a maqām, and the tonic of a Beethoven sonata are all centers in some meaningful sense, but their surrounding grammars are very different. Dahlhaus therefore distinguishes melodic forms of tonality from the specifically chord-centered “harmonic tonality” that becomes historically prominent later. citeturn3view0turn3view1turn9search1turn11search0

**Fixed or relatively stable pitch collection.** This means that a substantial passage draws predominantly from a bounded set of available pitches without continually replacing that set through chromatic harmonic modulation. “Fixed” should not be taken literally in all cases: instruments can be retuned between pieces, optional tones may exist, and performers may introduce inflections. A Javanese gamelan's pitches, for example, are physically fixed during performance, yet different gamelan sets can have substantially different tunings; an African lamellophone's tongues are tunable by sliding them; a kora has one sounding pitch per unstopped string but can be retuned between performances. citeturn13search4turn22search3turn25search3

**Ostinato** is a persistently recurring melodic, rhythmic, bass, or combined pattern. Recurrence does not imply mechanical sameness: an ostinato may support continuous variation. Mande *kumbengo* is an especially instructive case because the repeating framework and elaboratory playing are named practices rather than merely an outsider's detection of repetition. citeturn25search1

**Heterophony** describes simultaneous variants or elaborations of substantially the same melodic material; **polyphony** more broadly involves two or more simultaneous parts or melodic strands. Neither automatically entails a succession of functional chords. The reverse is also important: polyphony can yield perfectly audible triads and other vertical sonorities even when those sonorities are consequences of voice interaction rather than the primary units from which the music was generated. citeturn22search4turn3view1

**Counterpoint** concerns the regulated relation of simultaneous lines. **Organum**, in its medieval forms, is an early European category of polyphony whose procedures were based on intervallic and contrapuntal relations between voices; neither the existence of simultaneous intervals nor later analysts' ability to derive chord labels from them makes organum functional harmony. Medieval scholarship treats the tonal organization of twelfth- through fourteenth-century polyphony as historically distinct from mature tonal harmony. citeturn1search9

**Non-functional harmony** is a useful modern umbrella for genuine vertical sonorities whose succession is not governed primarily by tonic–predominant–dominant syntax. It includes some drone textures, planed or parallel sonorities, collections articulated as simultaneities, ostinato-generated verticalities, and explicitly chordal repertories whose chords behave according to other grammars. It must not become a residual category meaning “harmony that Western theory has failed to understand.” citeturn0search2turn27search15

Finally, **root movement does not equal functional progression**. A bass note can be a chord root, an inversion bass, a contrapuntal tone, a drone, a pedal, part of a repeating bass melody, or merely the lowest currently sounding member of an interlocking texture. Rameau's great conceptual move was precisely to distinguish the sounding bass from an abstract fundamental bass, demonstrating that even within functional harmonic theory “bass note” and “root” are not synonyms. citeturn27search0

### Ancient evidence is strong for organized pitch, weaker for “ancient drone systems”

One important correction to the original hypothesis follows from archaeology. We have genuinely old evidence for **systematic tuning and bounded pitch resources**. Mesopotamian cuneiform sources from around the early second millennium BCE have long been interpreted as procedures relating seven tunings or scales on stringed instruments. Jay Rahn's reconsideration of the evidence emphasizes that calling the system “diatonic” involves interpretative steps and that scholars need to guard against unconsciously translating Mesopotamian material into later European categories. citeturn5search3turn5search6

That is excellent evidence that sophisticated pitch organization is thousands of years older than common-practice tonality. It is **not equivalent evidence that drone organization is equally ancient**. Ancient lyres and harps demonstrate instruments with separately tuned strings and therefore bounded pitch possibilities, but an archaeological instrument's physical affordances cannot tell us by themselves whether performers sustained drones, alternated melodic formulas, played heterophonically, or produced repeated vertical sonorities. Recent scholarship on the Hurrian musical documents likewise stresses how much is at stake in reconstructing tuning, notation and performance from fragmentary texts. citeturn4search1turn5search1

Ancient Greek harmonic writings provide another counterexample to the idea that “pre-functional” must equal “drone.” Greek theorists developed elaborate intervallic and scalar thought over many centuries. The safest inference is therefore that **non-functional pitch organization can be extremely sophisticated without being drone-centered**. citeturn28search0turn28search2

### Medieval and Renaissance Europe: neither chordless nor functionally tonal

The usual textbook opposition—“medieval/Renaissance modality” followed abruptly by “Baroque tonality”—is too neat. Sixteenth-century counterpoint increasingly contains vertical sonorities easily recognizable as triads; composers controlled cadential destinations and large-scale pitch organization; and local passages can sometimes sound remarkably tonal to modern ears. Yet seventeenth-century historical change involved more than simply “discovering chords.” Dahlhaus locates the emergence of harmonic tonality in transformations in the relationship between chordal sonority, cadence, tonic orientation, rhythm and form. His criterion for full harmonic tonality is fundamentally **centering a system of chord relations on a tonic triad**, not the mere occurrence of thirds and fifths. citeturn3view0turn3view1

Recent Renaissance scholarship further complicates matters. Studies of Victoria use modal finals together with tonal types, cadential schemes and melodic organization; work on William Byrd stresses the importance of hexachordal and solmization structures; and Megan Kaes Long has questioned the assumption that the traditional “modal paradigm” exhaustively explains sixteenth-century polyphony. The appropriate historical conclusion is therefore not “Renaissance music had no harmony,” but **its harmony was embedded in a contrapuntal/modal grammar not yet identical with mature root-functional tonality**. citeturn1search7turn1search22turn1search2

### Why basso continuo matters—and why it is not yet Rameau

Basso continuo, practiced widely in Europe for approximately two centuries after about 1600, was a structural revolution because performers were given a bass and supplied other pitches above it according to figures, conventions and style. That procedure makes the bass extraordinarily consequential and encourages an increasingly vertical relation between upper voices and a supporting lower part. citeturn27search5

But thoroughbass thinking should not simply be equated with contemporary chord symbols. A figure tells a player which intervals should occur *above the sounding bass*. If the bass moves from C to E while the upper voices sustain C and G, the resulting sonority changes dramatically, yet a later root-theorist might hear both stages as different inversions or contrapuntal realizations of a larger harmonic entity. Conversely, several different chords can appear over one pedal bass. Rameau's *fundamental bass* is historically important precisely because it posits an **underlying root succession that may differ from the performed bass**—a conceptual layer not inherent in earlier thoroughbass notation. citeturn27search0turn0search22

Ground basses reinforce the same caution. European Baroque grounds could consist of repeating bass melodies or harmonic patterns and could support constantly changing upper parts; Grove notes that some forms changed historically in emphasis between harmonic ground and melodic bass. A repeating bass is therefore not automatically a string of modern harmonic “roots.” citeturn27search1

### How recent, exactly, is functional harmony?

Different answers follow from different definitions.

If “functional harmony” means **the mere existence of consonant vertical combinations**, it is very old and the hypothesis fails.

If it means **music with a tonal center and cadences**, it too is much older than the Baroque, and the hypothesis again needs qualification.

If it means **a mature major-minor system in which root-defined chords acquire systematic directional relationships to a tonic and support key change and large-scale tonal form**, then its decisive European consolidation lies approximately in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, with important precursors in the sixteenth. citeturn3view0turn3view1turn1search5

If it means **the explicitly theorized concept of chord function familiar from modern theory**, the history is later still: Rameau's root theory is eighteenth-century, *tonalité* in Choron's relevant sense is early nineteenth-century, and Riemannian function theory is a nineteenth-century codification. citeturn27search0turn27search13turn0search2

Thus a useful shorthand is:

**practice crystallizes c. 1600–1750; explicit modern theoretical codification follows and continues through the nineteenth century.**

That is considerably more precise than saying simply that “harmony was invented in the Baroque.”

## Comparative case studies

### Hindustani classical music: perhaps the clearest counterexample to chord-by-chord necessity

Hindustani classical music offers an especially strong case because its large-scale organization can be highly elaborate while its tonic remains strikingly stable. A contemporary scholarly overview describes rāg, cyclic tāl and a tonic-establishing drone as fundamental organizing principles and explicitly notes the absence of functional harmony “as such” and of ordinary Western-style key change. citeturn9search1

Yet rāga is not just a fixed scale laid over a drone. Richard Widdess's historical work and subsequent analytical scholarship treat rāga as a melodic system involving characteristic pitch behavior, hierarchy, phrase-types, ascent and descent practices, ornamentation and culturally specific processes of tonal organization. Applying a Western tonal grammar directly to Hindustani *ālāp* therefore requires substantial modification and testing rather than merely renaming pitches as scale degrees. citeturn7search12turn7search6

This tradition supports several parts of the hypothesis strongly: **stable pitch-world, sustained reference, melodic hierarchy, formula and extended development without functional chord succession**. It simultaneously weakens the hypothesis's “shifting gravity” component. The drone's tonic is normally intended to remain an extraordinarily strong reference, so Hindustani classical practice is not generally a case of continuously moving bass roots reinterpreting one fixed scale. citeturn9search1

There is also a striking historical caveat. Scholarship surveyed in *Rāgs Around the Clock* suggests that the near-omnipresence of the modern tonic drone may be much more historically recent than an easy “ancient Indian drone music” story would suggest, with iconographic, textual and instrumental evidence pointing to current-style drone practice becoming established perhaps no earlier than around the fifteenth century. Thus even an archetypal drone tradition has a history. citeturn9search1

### Arabic maqām: melodic tonality with genuine modulation

Arabic maqām provides a different model. Scott Marcus's field-based scholarship treats modulation as one of the central features of Arab music: performers can establish one maqām, move to another, and later return. The organizing logic is fundamentally melodic and includes characteristic pitch collections, focal pitches, melodic paths and smaller scalar segments commonly described as *ajnās*. citeturn11search0turn11search1turn10search0

This tradition strongly supports the proposition that **changing tonal gravity need not be Western harmonic modulation by chord progression**. A performer can redirect melodic attention and establish another maqām without anything analogous to a Beethovenian dominant preparing a new tonic chord. citeturn11search0

But maqām also demonstrates why “stable field, changing gravity” must not be universalized. Maqām modulation can require alterations in pitch inventory or changes of *jins*, not just different emphasis inside one immutable heptatonic collection. The better abstraction is **“melodic hierarchy and locally redirectable tonal orientation”**, not necessarily “same scale, different bass.” citeturn11search0turn10search0

### Central Javanese gamelan: one of the strongest analogues to stable field, changing gravity

Central Javanese music supplies probably the clearest cross-cultural analogue to part of the user's “stable field, changing gravity” idea, while also showing why native terminology matters. Javanese gamelan makes use of *sléndro* and *pélog* tuning systems; *sléndro* is five-tone and *pélog* has seven theoretical pitches, though practical pitch use depends on repertory and instrument. Crucially, there is no universal standardized frequency template: individual gamelan sets possess their own tunings. citeturn13search4turn24search0

Within a *laras*, the concept of *pathet* governs hierarchical treatment of pitches, melodic register, characteristic patterns and important points of repose. Experimental work explicitly describes hierarchical relationships among pitches within different *sléndro pathet*. Thus two passages can inhabit broadly the same tuning system yet distribute melodic weight differently. citeturn23search1turn24search6

This is unusually close to “same pitch-world, different gravity.” Even here, however, saying merely “the tonic changes” impoverishes the indigenous concept. *Pathet* affects acceptable melodic treatment, register and phrase behavior and cannot be reduced to moving a Western root beneath an unchanged scale. citeturn23search1turn24search9

Gamelan also demonstrates development through dimensions other than harmonic progression: cyclic structure, stratification of instrumental functions, elaboration of a core melodic layer, temporal density and interlocking techniques. Michael Tenzer's analysis of Balinese technique describes *kotekan*, in which paired parts interlock to produce rapid composite figuration. That technique is culturally and stylistically specific: Smithsonian documentation of a *semar pagulingan* repertory notes a case without the *kotekan* common elsewhere in Balinese gamelan, a useful warning against turning “interlocking gamelan” into a universal Indonesian stereotype. citeturn13search3turn13search12

### Mande music: kora, ostinato, tuning and variation

Mande music is particularly valuable because it undermines several popular generalizations at once. Eric Charry's major 2000 University of Chicago Press study, based on documentary research and fieldwork in Mali, Guinea, Senegal and The Gambia, documents diverse instruments, repertories, tunings and social contexts rather than a single generic “West African scale system.” citeturn15search10turn15search3

The kora is a twenty-one-string harp associated with Mande *jeli* traditions. It is **not properly described as an ancient pentatonic banjo ancestor**. Its tuning is generally heptatonic; its earliest known written description is Mungo Park's late-eighteenth-century account, and organological research cautions that oral origin stories cannot securely push the modern kora back to the medieval Mali Empire. Related Mande harps differ: *donsongoni* commonly uses pentatonic tuning, whereas *simbin/simbi* can be heptatonic. citeturn25search5turn25search3

A Cambridge study of Mande *jaliyaa* describes the recurring *kumbengo*—a term used for the ostinato/tuning framework—together with elaboratory *birimintingo*. This is an excellent instance of large-scale musical development arising from **a repeating pitch/rhythmic framework plus continuous variation**, rather than from a need to replace the underlying chord every beat or measure. citeturn25search1

Kora organology complicates simplistic anti-harmonic claims as well. Each unstopped string gives one pitch, and the physical alternation of scale tones across the two hands makes rapid scalar patterns and consonant simultaneous combinations highly idiomatic. Lucy Durán notes that adjacent-string gestures can yield triadic-sounding combinations. Thus Mande performance can be “harmonic” in the ordinary acoustic sense without thereby becoming an application of European tonic–predominant–dominant grammar. citeturn25search3

Calling every low kora note a “root” would consequently be methodologically risky. Low strings can participate in a cyclic texture, create local intervallic backgrounds and reinforce repeated patterns, but one needs repertory-specific evidence before interpreting each as the generator of a chord. citeturn25search1turn25search5

### Aka and BaAka forest polyphonies: complexity from recurrent interacting lines

The vocal polyphony of Aka communities in Central Africa is among the clearest counterexamples to a chord-first model. Simha Arom's research established the exceptional contrapuntal and polyrhythmic complexity of Aka ensemble practice, and subsequent work by Susanne Fürniss analyzes recurring formulas, variation and the organization of interdependent parts. UNESCO's documentation likewise emphasizes orally transmitted polyphonic singing with substantial scope for variation and spontaneous expression. citeturn16search1turn16search4turn16search2

The important analytical point is not that “there are no chords.” At any instant, several singers inevitably produce vertical interval structures. The stronger claim is that **vertical sonorities need not be the privileged generative units**. A chordal reduction can tell an outsider which pitch classes coincide, but it can obscure the fact that the music's continuity and complexity arise through cyclic, overlapping, rhythmically differentiated parts and ongoing variation. citeturn16search4turn16search14

Michelle Kisliuk's extended fieldwork among BaAka communities further demonstrates why the music should be understood in its performance context—song, dance, participation, social relation and bodily movement—rather than extracted as an exotic “harmonic system.” citeturn17search1turn17search9

This case supports **repetition + interlock + independent line + emergent verticality** much more directly than it supports “stable scale + moving bass roots.”

### Appalachian old-time music: drones and open resonance are real; “stable scale over roots” is less securely historical

Appalachian music requires unusually careful treatment because twentieth-century narratives often portrayed it as an isolated survival of British or “Celtic” antiquity. Historical and ethnographic work instead reveals a region shaped by multiple streams of interaction, including British Isles ballad and fiddle traditions, African and African American instrument and performance practices, commercial music, minstrel-stage repertories, dance traditions, recording and radio. Library of Congress collections preserve extensive old-time fiddle repertory, while scholarship on southern Appalachian dance and string-band history stresses its complex, intercultural formation. citeturn19search0turn19search15turn19search8turn19search23

The **drone/open-string portion of the hypothesis is strongly documented**. Alan Jabbour's fiddle transcriptions explicitly record double stops and drone notes where they are structurally significant, while comparative scholarship on Southern American fiddle style describes open-string and drone practices. Alternate or “cross” tunings allow resonant open strings to reinforce particular pitch centers and intervals. citeturn20search15turn20search26

The five-string banjo adds another literal drone affordance: its shortened fifth string commonly produces a high repeated pitch rather than functioning like an ordinary bass-to-treble guitar string. Traditional alternate tunings can also suppress or leave ambiguous the third, creating textures modern listeners may describe as “modal,” “open” or “suspended.” citeturn20search25turn20search22

But the more specific proposition that old Appalachian musicians systematically conceived **one unchanged upper scale over a sequence of deliberately changing bass roots** is substantially harder to document. Surviving practice strongly supports melody-centered playing, drone resonance, alternate tuning and modal melodic collections; those facts are compatible with modern musicians hearing changing chord-like meanings against accompaniment, but they do not by themselves prove that “stable scale over roots” was an indigenous theoretical principle. The safest classification of that interpretation is **plausible modern analytical reinterpretation**, not established historical fact. citeturn20search15turn20search26

### Africa and the banjo: strong ancestry, wrong instrument if one says “kora became banjo”

The African ancestry of the banjo is now strongly documented, but the historically responsible story is one of **a family of West African plucked lutes, diasporic reconstruction and creolization**, not “the kora turned into the banjo.” Smithsonian historical work describes enslaved Africans and their descendants in the Caribbean combining features of African lutes with elements encountered in the Atlantic world, producing early gourd-bodied, skin-headed banjos before the standardized nineteenth-century American form. citeturn18search0

The Jola *akonting/ekonting* of Senegal and The Gambia is especially relevant: it is a three-stringed gourd-bodied lute, and Smithsonian Folkways research highlights its structural and playing affinities with early banjos. It is also a living Jola tradition that should not be reduced to the status of a museum-piece “missing link.” citeturn18search3turn18search12

The *akonting* and early banjo share particularly suggestive features including a shortened drone string and downstroke playing procedures. Yet scholars have identified numerous West African lute traditions potentially relevant to New World banjo development, making a single-instrument genealogy unwarranted. citeturn18search0turn20search22

The kora, by contrast, is a bridge harp with twenty-one strings and belongs to a different organological family. Mande lutes such as *ngoni*-type instruments and Jola spike lutes are structurally closer candidates for comparison with the banjo than the kora. citeturn25search5turn18search0

What may plausibly have persisted or been transformed across the Atlantic are **instrumental principles**—short drone strings, rhythmic downstroke articulation, cyclic accompaniment, interweaving of melody and rhythm, timbral ideals and approaches to tuning—not an intact West African “harmonic system” transplanted unchanged into Appalachia. The historical record supports continuity plus transformation, not one-to-one survival. citeturn18search0turn18search3

## Fixed-pitch instruments, stable fields, bass, and local versus global relations

### What instrument construction actually tells us

Organology provides unusually concrete evidence for the hypothesis, but only if physical affordance is separated from musical grammar.

A fixed-pitch instrument can make particular collections highly available. A kora string produces one pitch unless retuned; a gamelan metallophone has a bounded set of keyed pitches; a lamellophone has tuned tongues; a zither may assign some strings specifically to drones. Such construction makes some combinations easy and others impossible during a given performance. citeturn25search3turn13search4turn22search3

Yet **affordance is not destiny**. The Met's nyonganyonga, for example, is retuned by sliding its metal tongues, so its pitch set is stable during playing without being immutable across its life. Javanese gamelan sets are intentionally ensemble-specific rather than copies of one mathematically fixed “sléndro scale.” Kora tuning can be changed, and modern mechanisms have expanded performers' retuning capabilities. citeturn22search3turn13search4turn25search5

Some instruments do much more strongly encode drone texture. A nineteenth-century *épinette des Vosges* in the Metropolitan Museum has two melody strings and three dedicated drone strings; a French eighteenth-century hurdy-gurdy has melody strings alongside several continuously wheel-bowed drone strings; historical bagpipes similarly combine chanters with drones. Here it is not speculative to say that the instrument itself creates a stable acoustic reference. citeturn22search1turn22search5turn22search7

Other fixed-pitch instruments undermine the equation **fixed scale = drone**. The Japanese *shō* mouth organ can sound several free reeds simultaneously; sustained pitch-clusters or chords are a distinctive feature of gagaku. This is genuinely chordal texture, but it should not therefore be redescribed as Western functional harmony. citeturn22search2

Thus the organological inference should have three steps:

**document the available pitches → document actual performance technique → only then infer compositional consequences.**

Skipping the middle step is precisely the error the user's methodological rules correctly warn against.

### Pentatonic systems are not underdeveloped harmonic systems

Restricted pitch collections can support enormous formal complexity. The five-tone *sléndro* field of Java can be differentiated by *pathet*, register, melodic formula, temporal density, cyclic punctuation and multiple simultaneously elaborating instrumental layers. citeturn23search1turn24search0turn24search6

The pentatonic *donsongoni* repertory of Mande hunters differs fundamentally from the heptatonic kora tradition despite occurring in a partly overlapping cultural world. That fact alone should make “African pentatonic music” an unacceptable analytical category. citeturn25search3turn25search5

With five pitch classes, musical development can arise through melodic permutation, rhythm, register, timbre, variation, cyclic recombination, entrances and exits, contrapuntal density and shifting hierarchy. Nothing in music theory requires a new pitch class or chord root for every new formal event. Javanese, Mande and Central African cases supply different demonstrations of that principle. citeturn13search3turn25search1turn16search4

A Western musician can certainly look at some resulting sonorities and say “sus2,” “add6,” “quartal,” “minor 11 without the third,” and so forth. Those labels can be useful **acoustic shorthand** if explicitly identified as etic descriptions. They become misleading when converted into claims that the performers are mentally progressing through those chord symbols. citeturn3view1turn25search3

### “Stable field, changing gravity”: common enough to be useful, not universal enough to be an ancient master principle

The evidence suggests four importantly different situations.

**Stable field, stable center:** Hindustani rāga with tonic drone is the clearest case. The pitch hierarchy and melodic behavior can evolve greatly while the reference tonic remains stable. citeturn9search1

**Stable or broadly stable field, changing hierarchical emphasis:** Javanese *laras/pathet* comes closest to the proposed model. The same tuning environment can support different hierarchical distributions, characteristic phrase endings and registral practices. citeturn23search1turn24search6

**Changing center with partially changing pitch material:** Arabic maqām modulation gives real redirection of tonal focus, but the available pitches or governing *jins* may change along with the focus. citeturn11search0turn10search0

**Cyclic/polyphonic organization where “center” may not be the most informative category:** Aka polyphony illustrates why some music is better understood through the patterned relations among parts than by searching for a succession of centers. citeturn16search1turn16search4

Consequently, “stable field, changing gravity” is **a real recurrent structural possibility**, not merely a modern fantasy. But its best evidence is tradition-specific, and the exact mechanism of “gravity” varies—*pathet*, melodic focal notes, finals, register, drone, low repeated tone, local cadence, or some combination. There is no evidence that all these are manifestations of one historically continuous system. citeturn23search1turn11search0turn9search1

### Global pitch status versus local interval relation

The user's distinction between **global function** and **local function** is theoretically productive, but the word *function* should probably be replaced outside Western functional theory.

A safer pair is:

**global pitch status** = a pitch's role within the larger field, mode, rāga, maqām, *pathet*, tuning or melodic hierarchy;

**local interval relation** = that pitch's momentary relationship to a drone, current low note, temporary focal pitch, neighboring line or simultaneous sonority.

These two layers are perceptually distinguishable in principle. Research on Western listeners shows that the same physical pitch is interpreted partly through its tonal position established by preceding harmonic context, while pitch hierarchies are also induced by distribution, duration and learned statistical regularities. citeturn26search6turn26search3turn26search29

Imagine a fixed pitch **E** while the lower sounding note moves C → D → A. Acoustically E changes from a major third above C, to a major second above D, to a perfect fifth above A. Those local intervals are objective. What is *not* objective is that the listener must therefore hear **C major → Dsus2 → A5**. That second step introduces a culturally learned chord ontology.

In a genuinely field-centered system, E can remain “the same member of the global pitch world” while acquiring changing local intervallic color. That is a legitimate analytical phenomenon. Whether performers themselves foreground that distinction must be established separately through native terminology, performance practice, pedagogy or ethnographic report. citeturn26search0turn26search29

Javanese *pathet* is particularly suggestive because pitch hierarchy and phrase-ending behavior change while the broader tuning remains; maqām supplies an analogous global/local distinction through large-scale maqām identity and locally active *jins*; rāga supplies stable tonic-relative pitch identities together with phrase-level melodic functions. But none of these cultures requires the exact proposed binary “global function/local function.” That binary is best treated as a **comparative analytical tool**, not a discovered universal indigenous theory. citeturn23search1turn11search0turn9search1

### Bass movement is not synonymous with chord change

The proposition “the bass moves, therefore the harmony has changed chords” is indeed historically contingent.

Even within common-practice theory it is false in literal form. Because inversion allows different bass notes beneath one chord root, the actual bass can move while the underlying harmonic root remains constant. Rameau's fundamental-bass theory formalized precisely this distinction. citeturn27search0

Before root theory became normative, basses could be conceived in other ways. Figured bass instructs performers in intervals above the sounding bass; counterpoint can make a lower line one participant among several; a ground bass can be a repeating melodic scaffold; a pedal may remain while the upper sonority changes; a drone does not cease being a drone whenever the melody makes another interval against it. citeturn27search5turn27search1

Outside that historical system, the identification becomes even less secure. A low kora note in a *kumbengo* can be a recurring element of the pattern; a low metallophone or gong tone in gamelan can articulate structural time; a banjo's fifth-string drone can sound above the melody rather than below it. “Bass” itself is therefore partly a registral description, whereas “root” is a theoretical claim. citeturn25search1turn24search0turn20search25

This yields a crucial methodological distinction:

> **A change in the lowest sounding pitch guarantees a change in local sonority. It does not guarantee a change of harmonic root, harmonic function, mode, tonal center, or pitch field.**

That statement is true even inside Western tonal theory and becomes still more important in comparative analysis. citeturn27search0

## Perception, scale-degree identity, analytical lenses, and repetition

### Which intervals most strongly define harmonic identity?

There is no culture-free ranking of “harmonic importance” that applies identically to all musical systems. Music perception reflects both auditory constraints and learned cultural schemas. McDermott's review of pitch perception emphasizes exactly this interaction, and cross-cultural/learning research shows that tonal schemas are substantially acquired from exposure. citeturn26search0turn26search29

Within **Western major-minor tonality**, however, the user's intuition that some scale degrees are more identity-defining than others is well founded.

**Root.** The root is maximally important once the listener is operating in a root-based chord grammar: it defines the abstract identity under inversion and places the chord in a tonal network. But a root is a theoretical/cognitive property, not necessarily the same thing as the lowest pitch. citeturn27search0

**Fifth.** Root plus perfect fifth strongly establishes an open consonant frame, aided by a simple harmonic relationship. But because C–G contains neither E nor E♭, it leaves major/minor quality unspecified. This makes the open fifth unusually useful for textures intended to preserve modal or harmonic ambiguity. General research on harmonicity and consonance supports an auditory contribution to the salience of such intervallic relationships, although musical interpretation remains culturally conditioned. citeturn26search0turn26search15

**Third.** In Western triadic grammar, the third is exceptionally identity-defining because the contrast C–E versus C–E♭ is precisely what distinguishes major from minor tonic triads. Major and minor triads also produce robust perceptual and neural pitch-salience responses in laboratory work. Thus sustaining the third makes mode quality difficult to ignore in a way that sustaining a perfect fifth does not. citeturn26search12turn26search15

**Second/ninth.** A second above a presumed root does not by itself determine major versus minor. Under Western chord hearing it can be labeled an added ninth or suspension, but in a modal/drone texture it may simply be one recurring member of the field. Its “suspended” quality is therefore context-dependent.

**Fourth/eleventh.** The fourth likewise leaves major/minor identity underdetermined. Its status has changed dramatically across Western contrapuntal and harmonic contexts; treating every fourth over a bass as “sus4” retroactively imports a modern chord label where an older musician might have understood a contrapuntal interval.

**Sixth.** A sixth can be highly diagnostic of *modal environment*: relative to D, B-natural differentiates Dorian-type material from B-flat in an Aeolian-type collection. Yet it need not define the quality of a tonic chord. Thus the sixth can be globally identity-rich while locally harmonically noncommittal.

**Seventh.** In Western common-practice tonality, a semitone below the tonic acquires exceptional directional force as a leading tone. That force is reinforced by learned tonal syntax and statistical expectation; it should not be treated as an acoustically inevitable universal. Flattening the seventh can strongly alter modal identity and reduce conventional dominant–tonic implication. Research showing culture-dependent acquisition of tonal schemas is important here. citeturn26search29turn26search6

A useful compositional implication follows: **root/fifth, second and fourth can often maintain a relatively open tonal identity, whereas a sustained third and strongly directed seventh tend to specify Western major/minor interpretation much more quickly.** Sixth degree behavior can be especially powerful for modal differentiation. That statement is most defensible as a claim about Western-enculturated hearing, not a human universal. citeturn26search0turn26search29

### A brief melodic third versus a sustained harmonic third

The user's proposition that an identity-defining pitch may behave differently when briefly melodic rather than sustained vertically is also defensible, but should be expressed probabilistically rather than as a hard psychoacoustic law.

Experiments on tonal hierarchy show that **duration and frequency of occurrence influence perceived pitch importance**: tones receiving longer duration can acquire greater hierarchical weight. Other studies demonstrate that preceding tonal context changes how a later pitch is represented and judged. citeturn26search3turn26search6

Consequently, an E-natural passing rapidly through a D-centered melody can contribute Dorian color without necessarily making a listener hear a sustained “D major/minor chord identity.” The same pitch held continuously against D, especially together with A and in a chordal timbre, receives more temporal salience and stronger vertical grouping and is therefore more likely to be heard as a major third defining a D-major-type sonority by a Western listener. That conclusion is an inference from duration, tonal-context and chord-salience research rather than evidence for a universal threshold at which “melody becomes harmony.” citeturn26search3turn26search12turn26search6

This distinction has major compositional value. A musician can retain an identity-defining pitch as a **transient melodic event** while withholding it from sustained resonant strata, allowing the larger sonority to remain more ambiguous than the melody itself.

### Drone-based ear organization

A drone changes the listening problem. Instead of continuously asking “what chord is this?”, the listener can hear each melodic pitch as a changing interval against one stable reference. The same drone produces a succession of unisons, seconds, thirds, fourths, fifths, sixths and sevenths without requiring the reference itself to lose its status. Indian classical music provides a highly developed real-world example of tonic-relative hearing of this kind. citeturn9search1

This does not mean listeners are prevented from perceiving emergent chords. Vertical grouping is a normal auditory possibility, and culturally Western listeners may spontaneously hear chordal implications in any sufficiently sustained combination. Rather, the important distinction is that **a chordal interpretation need not be the organizing principle that generated the music**. citeturn26search0turn3view1

Repeated exposure also induces hierarchy. Long duration, recurrent appearance and structurally emphasized placement all influence how tones become perceptually privileged. This gives a perceptual basis for why a drone, final, repeated low pitch or recurrent phrase-ending note can establish “gravity” even without dominant–tonic progression. citeturn26search3turn26search24

### Does triadic thinking distort older and non-Western music?

**Sometimes—and sometimes it is genuinely illuminating.**

A vertical chord label answers a legitimate descriptive question: *which pitches are simultaneous, and what intervals do they form?* For comparing sonority, register, consonance, dissonance or voice-leading, that can be extremely useful.

The distortion occurs when the analyst silently changes the question to: *which root-generated chord was the composer or performer intending, and what Western harmonic function did it perform?*

Dahlhaus's historical warning is decisive here: vertical configurations in fourteenth- through sixteenth-century polyphony cannot automatically be interpreted according to the later grammar in which chords themselves articulate the mode or key. Documentary and compositional evidence is needed to establish that chord identity was conceptually primary. citeturn3view0turn3view1

The same issue appears in ethnomusicology. Contemporary discussion of African harmony illustrates a productive tension: scholars have criticized Eurocentric assumptions, but simply declaring harmony “inapplicable” to African repertories can be equally reductive when musicians demonstrably coordinate simultaneous pitches. The correct response is not to ban Western analytical tools but to distinguish **description from cultural explanation** and to test analytical categories against repertory, pedagogy and musicians' concepts. citeturn27search6

Thus:

**“C–D–G sounds simultaneously”** is descriptive.

**“That is Csus2”** is a useful Western shorthand if clearly labeled.

**“The musicians are performing a tonic suspended-second chord”** is an ethnographic/historical claim requiring additional evidence.

This distinction is central to responsible comparative musicology.

### Repetition does not imply simplicity

The hypothesis is especially strong where it treats repeated material as a **generator of complexity** rather than its opposite.

Mande *kumbengo* supports ongoing elaboration and variation; Aka polyphony layers recurrent formulas with rhythmic differentiation and improvisational change; gamelan interlocking creates composite patterns more rapid or intricate than individual performers' components. citeturn25search1turn16search4turn13search3

This mechanism can be generalized without claiming historical lineage:

> a small inventory of events + recurrence + relative displacement + variation + changing density = a potentially enormous perceptual state-space.

Modern minimalism provides a useful structural comparison, but historical claims must be controlled carefully. Steve Reich formulated his interest in audible gradual process in **1968**, before traveling to Ghana in **1970** to study drumming. Reich's own account confirms his Ghana study, and scholarship on *Drumming* stresses that African experience confirmed or redirected existing interests rather than supplying his phase technique wholesale. citeturn28search14turn28search3turn28search9

It is therefore accurate to compare repeated-cell construction, additive processes, interlocking and emergent composite rhythm across Reich and selected African traditions. It is inaccurate to describe Aka or Ewe music as “ancient minimalism,” or Reich's phase shifting as simply an African technique. citeturn28search9turn28search11

## What the hypothesis gets right, where it overreaches, and a refined model

### What it gets right

The hypothesis correctly identifies a widespread analytical bias: musicians trained primarily in modern chord-symbol practice can habitually parse every change in the lowest note as a chord change and every simultaneity as a root-bearing chord. Historical theory itself shows that this is not a neutral way to read earlier European polyphony. citeturn3view0turn3view1turn27search0

It correctly recognizes that **functional tonality is historically particular rather than the default state of music**. The gradual rise of tonic-centered chordal systems in seventeenth-century Europe, the emergence of fundamental-bass theory in the early eighteenth century, and nineteenth-century codification of tonality/function can be historically traced. citeturn27search0turn27search13turn0search2

It correctly identifies **drone, modal hierarchy, cyclic repetition, formula, ostinato, heterophony, counterpoint and interlocking** as genuine primary organizing principles in many traditions. citeturn9search1turn13search3turn25search1turn16search4

It correctly intuits that **pitch identity is multilevel**: a note may belong stably to a larger field while its immediate acoustic relation to another sounding pitch changes. Perceptual research supports the broader proposition that pitch identity depends both on learned global tonal organization and on immediate context. citeturn26search6turn26search29

It also correctly sees that **harmonic complexity can emerge rather than being composed as a sequence of chords**. Independent lines, ostinati and interlocking cells necessarily generate verticalities, and those verticalities may be sonically rich even when nobody has selected them as “Chord X followed by Chord Y.” citeturn16search4turn13search3turn3view1

### Where it overreaches

The first overreach is the phrase **“before functional harmony.”** There was no universal global “before” followed by a universal “after.” Functional tonality emerged in a particular European history while many other systems continued alongside it, and European vernacular, sacred and art repertories themselves remained diverse.

The second is treating **drones, modes, pentatonicism, ostinati and interlocking as if they naturally belonged together**. They do not. Hindustani rāga may combine modal hierarchy and drone but not pentatonicism necessarily; Aka polyphony combines cycles and independent parts without being adequately described as drone music; a Japanese *shō* produces sustained chords; Mande kora is ordinarily heptatonic rather than pentatonic; Javanese gamelan combines fixed instruments with *pathet* and cyclic organization according to its own system. citeturn9search1turn16search4turn22search2turn25search5turn23search1

The third is assuming **fixed-pitch instruments imply primitive or static pitch thought**. Ancient tuning texts, gamelan tuning practice and kora/lamellophone organology show that fixed instruments can encode highly sophisticated tuning choices and can be retuned. citeturn5search6turn13search4turn22search3

The fourth is making **“changing bass roots”** too central. That mechanism is far less universal than the broader phenomena of changing emphasis, register, phrase ending, melodic focal point or low-pitch relation. The historical and comparative evidence supports “variable reference and local interval context” much more robustly than “universal ancient root motion under a fixed scale.”

The fifth is treating “pentatonic” as synonymous with “old.” Ancient Mesopotamian evidence has long been interpreted in heptatonic terms; the kora is heptatonic; many ancient and traditional systems are not pentatonic. Conversely, pentatonic systems remain fully contemporary. citeturn5search3turn5search6turn25search5

The sixth is describing Appalachian modality as a transparent survival of Celtic music. British and Irish/Scottish streams are significant, but Appalachian musical history is also profoundly entangled with African American, commercial and transregional musical practice. Banjo history alone makes a monocultural genealogy untenable. citeturn19search15turn19search23turn18search0

### A refined theoretical model

The user's proposed model is:

> one drone or stable tone + bass/root movement + relatively stable pitch field + selective identity-defining tones + repeated melodic/rhythmic cells.

As a **modern compositional framework**, it is excellent.

As the description of **one historical older harmonic system**, it is not defensible.

Its individual components have different histories:

| Component | Historical status |
|---|---|
| Stable drone/reference pitch | Widespread and old in many traditions, but not universal; securely documented examples differ enormously in date and culture. citeturn22search1turn22search5turn9search1 |
| Relatively stable pitch field | Extremely widespread and demonstrably ancient as a general possibility; ancient tuning evidence reaches back millennia. citeturn5search3turn5search6 |
| Modal/pitch hierarchy | Widespread but realized through culturally different concepts such as medieval mode, rāga, maqām and *pathet*. citeturn1search9turn9search1turn11search0turn23search1 |
| Repeated melodic/rhythmic cells | Extremely widespread; strongly documented in Mande and Central African repertories and central to many cyclic traditions. citeturn25search1turn16search4 |
| Interlocking parts | Strongly documented in particular African and Indonesian traditions, not a universal premodern trait. citeturn13search3turn16search4 |
| Changing bass beneath stable upper field | Real and musically plausible, but much less well supported as a universal historical organizing principle. |
| Bass note treated as chord root | Specifically associated with certain chordal theoretical frameworks and cannot be assumed cross-culturally. citeturn27search0 |
| Selectively withholding thirds/sevenths to preserve ambiguity | Highly effective modern compositional technique; historical analogues exist as texture or scale behavior, but the explicit theory of “identity-defining tones” is largely a modern synthesis. citeturn26search3turn26search29 |

A better name for the overall synthesis would therefore be **stable-field / variable-reference composition**, or, when the actual musical features warrant it, a **modal-drone-ostinato framework**.

“Field-centered organization” is useful because it avoids claiming that every simultaneous collection constitutes a chord. “Variable reference” is preferable to “root movement” because a low or emphasized pitch need not be a chord root. “Local interval relation” is preferable to “local harmonic function” outside functional tonal contexts.

A theoretically precise version of the framework might be written:

> **Maintain a relatively stable pitch field; establish one or more persistent reference pitches; create form through recurrent cells, cyclic rhythm, texture, register and density; permit low-register or focal pitches to alter the local intervallic interpretation of field members; and use culturally or stylistically salient pitches selectively to sharpen or blur local identity.**

That model has numerous historical analogues in its component processes, but **the combination is a contemporary comparative synthesis**.

### Implications for contemporary composition and improvisation

A contemporary musician can draw profound practical lessons from this research without pretending to recreate any single historical culture.

One can begin with a pitch field rather than a chord chart. Instead of asking “what is the next chord?”, ask **which pitches belong to the world of the piece, which are stable, which are rare, and which have identity-defining power?** Javanese *pathet*, rāga and maqām all demonstrate—differently—that a pitch collection becomes musically meaningful through hierarchy and behavior, not merely membership. citeturn23search1turn9search1turn11search0

A second strategy is to separate **resonance layer from melodic layer**. Sustain tonic/fifth, drone or another neutral dyad while allowing the melody to introduce thirds, sixths or sevenths transiently. The perceptual research discussed above predicts that short-duration tones can contribute melodic identity without acquiring the same salience as sustained structural pitches. citeturn26search3turn26search6

A third is to let bass motion alter **local interval color** without automatically rewriting the global field. Over a stable collection such as D–E–F–G–A–B–C, low D, C, G and A produce markedly different vertical interpretations. A composer can exploit those differences while refusing to decide that every bass note constitutes a new chord root.

A fourth is to create development through **permutation instead of harmonic replacement**: rotate a cell; displace an attack; omit one event; double another; transfer it between registers; alter density; stagger entrances; interlock complementary patterns; change which note ends the phrase. Mande, Aka and gamelan repertories demonstrate that repeated structures can support extraordinary complexity, while Reich demonstrates a later, independently formulated compositional exploration of audibly unfolding process. citeturn25search1turn16search4turn13search3turn28search14

A fifth is to treat chord labels as an **optional readout rather than the generative source**. After composing a texture, one can ask what a Western listener might call each sonority. That can be useful for rehearsal or arranging. But there is no need to let that retrospective naming determine the composition.

Responsible intercultural practice means being equally explicit about provenance. A musician inspired by *kotekan* should learn what *kotekan* actually is rather than calling all interlocking “Balinese.” A musician inspired by kora should distinguish kora from *ngoni* and *akonting*. A composer inspired by rāga should not claim that choosing seven pitches constitutes composing a rāga. Structural inspiration is legitimate; claiming cultural authenticity requires much more knowledge and relationship. citeturn13search3turn25search5turn18search3turn9search1

## Final answer

**Yes—with substantial qualifications.**

It is historically and theoretically defensible to say that **modern Western chord-by-chord functional thinking is only one relatively recent way of organizing pitch**, provided “functional” is defined narrowly enough to mean a system of root-bearing harmonies with directed roles relative to a tonic rather than the mere existence of chords, consonances, tonal centers, cadences or bass motion. That specific system crystallized gradually in European music principally during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries; its root-based theoretical articulation is strongly associated with Rameau from 1722 onward, while the terminology and explicit theory of tonality and harmonic function were further systematized in the nineteenth century. citeturn3view0turn3view1turn27search0turn27search13turn0search2

It is also defensible to say that **much older and globally widespread musical practices have organized pitch through other principles**, including stable reference pitches, modal or melodic hierarchies, restricted or relatively stable collections, drones, formulaic melodies, cyclic rhythm, ostinati, heterophony, counterpoint, interlocking textures, register and shifting pitch emphasis. Ancient Mesopotamian tuning evidence shows highly organized pitch systems almost four millennia ago; medieval and Renaissance European music demonstrates sophisticated non-functional tonal and contrapuntal organization; and living traditions in South Asia, the Middle East, Africa and Indonesia demonstrate numerous fully developed alternatives to common-practice chord syntax. citeturn5search3turn5search6turn1search9turn9search1turn11search0turn13search3turn25search1turn16search4

The required qualifications are exacting. **There was never one universal “older harmonic system.”** Drone, modality, pentatonicism, interlocking and ostinato do not necessarily occur together. “Ancient” does not mean “pentatonic” or “drone-based.” A stable scale does not imply a stable tonic, and a changing tonic does not imply an unchanged scale. A moving bass is not necessarily a moving chord root. A vertical triad does not necessarily imply triadic compositional thought. Modern labels such as *sus4*, *add9*, *minor 11* or Roman numerals can be useful retrospective descriptions but cannot be treated as native concepts without evidence. Indian rāga, Arabic maqām, Javanese *pathet*, Mande *kumbengo*, Aka polyphony and Appalachian drone practice are structurally different phenomena, not regional dialects of one primordial system. citeturn9search1turn11search0turn23search1turn25search1turn16search4turn20search15

Most importantly, the specific model **“one stable scale over changing bass roots” is not established as a globally ancient organizing system**. Strong analogues exist—particularly where a persistent pitch world supports changing local pitch hierarchies or where repeated low tones alter local intervallic relationships—but the comparative evidence supports the broader concept of **stable or bounded pitch fields with variable local emphasis and interval context** much better than it supports universal root motion. Javanese *pathet* is one of the closest documented analogues; Hindustani rāga illustrates stable field with mostly stable center; maqām illustrates mobile centers with partially mutable fields; Appalachian practice securely documents drones and resonant tuning but not a native universal theory of “scale over changing roots.” citeturn23search1turn9search1turn11search0turn20search15

The most historically defensible final formulation is therefore:

> **Root-functional major-minor harmony is one historically specific, comparatively recent solution to the problem of organizing pitch. Human musical cultures—including pre-tonal European cultures—have developed many other solutions in which musical coherence is carried principally or substantially by melodic and modal hierarchy, stable or restricted pitch resources, drones and pedals, contrapuntal interaction, recurring formulas and ostinati, cyclic rhythm, heterophony, interlocking parts, register, timbre, and changing points of pitch emphasis. Some of these practices allow a relatively stable global pitch world to coexist with changing local interval relationships or centers of gravity. They should be compared as recurrent structural possibilities, not collapsed into a single ancient, non-Western, or pre-functional harmonic system.** citeturn3view0turn3view1turn9search1turn23search1turn25search1turn16search4turn26search0
